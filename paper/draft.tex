\begin{abstract}
Adjective ordering preferences, such as placing size before colour, have been explained by two accounts of communicative efficiency.
Hierarchical composition favours placing less subjective adjectives closer to the noun, where they restrict the interpretation of more subjective ones in the complete utterance; incremental pragmatics favours placing more discriminating adjectives earlier, where each adjective helps the listener narrow down the referent sooner.
However, the two accounts can favour opposing orders when the scene makes the less subjective adjective the more discriminating one.
Do speakers resolve this conflict by evaluating the complete utterance, or each adjective as they produce it?
We develop Rational Speech Act production models that formalise both accounts and span this range, from a global to an incremental speaker.
Between these endpoints, a plan-guided speaker chooses each adjective for its immediate usefulness while looking ahead to the complete utterance, so that both pressures can influence adjective order.
In German production data, speakers adapted which adjectives they mentioned to scene variation, while their order largely followed a stable convention.
We found that the plan-guided speaker best predicted these data, whereas modelling hierarchical composition added no predictive benefit.
Simulations nevertheless showed that composition can favour size-first order, but only under restricted conditions.
Together, these findings motivate an account of boundedly rational communication in which partial planning supports good-enough production.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} adjective ordering, communicative efficiency, reference production, Rational Speech Act, Bayesian cognitive modelling
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\label{sec:background}
% =============================================================================

Adjective ordering provides a window onto how stable order preferences interact with what is informative in a particular scene.
In languages with prenominal modification, robust ordering preferences favour size-first sequences such as \emph{the big blue sticker} over \emph{the blue big sticker} \citep{sproat1991,trotzke2019,scontras2023adjective}.
Communicative efficiency has been proposed as an explanation for both stable adjective-order preferences and their sensitivity to referential context \citep{scontras2017subjectivity,franke2019subjectivity,futrell-etal-2020-determines}.

\label{sec:bg:subjectivity}
But what counts as efficient---and therefore early---for the listener in a complex referential setting?
Two accounts give different answers, one based on hierarchical composition and one on incremental pragmatics.
Information-theoretic accounts additionally relate ordering to incremental processing under memory limits \citep{Hahn_information_2018,futrell-etal-2020-determines,futrell2023information}.
We focus on composition and incremental pragmatics because they make opposite predictions in referential scenes and can be formalised within the same speaker model.
The first account, hierarchical composition, starts from the observation that more subjective adjectives tend to occur farther from the noun, which yields a size-first preference for size and colour \citep{scontras2017subjectivity,trainin2021s}.
On this account, the noun-adjacent adjective restricts the context in which an outer adjective is interpreted \citep{franke2019subjectivity,scontras2019grammatical,scontras2020incremental}.
In \emph{the big blue sticker}, \emph{blue} combines with \emph{sticker} before \emph{big}, potentially narrowing the comparison class against which size is evaluated.
Because colour is less subjective than size, evaluating \emph{big} among the blue objects can reduce uncertainty about its interpretation.

The second account, incremental pragmatics, concerns the order in which the listener receives information.
Listeners use partial linguistic input and visual context to restrict candidate referents before a noun phrase is complete \citep{tanenhaus1995integration,sedivy1999achieving}.
Placing the more discriminating property first can facilitate this process, favouring colour-first order when colour identifies the target sooner \citep{fukumura2018ordering,rubio2020incrementality,rubio2021wordorder}.
The two accounts therefore conflict when the less subjective property is also the more discriminating one.
In a scene where colour alone identifies the target, composition still favours \emph{the big blue sticker}, whereas incremental pragmatics favours \emph{the blue big sticker}.
The compositional benefit of size-first order depends on the complete phrase, whereas the incremental benefit of colour-first order depends on each adjective as it is produced.

\label{sec:bg:efficiency}
However, there is another way of looking at this conflict, and the competition between size-first and colour-first order may never arise in the first place.
Whether either order is produced depends first on which adjectives are selected for an utterance.
In a colour-sufficient scene, \emph{blue} in \emph{the blue sticker} may complete the utterance alone, so that size and colour need not be ordered at all.
Speakers may nevertheless add \emph{big}, even though it is redundant.
Redundancy also occurs in size-sufficient scenes, where speakers may produce \emph{the big blue sticker} although size alone identifies the target.
Redundant colour, however, is far more common than redundant size, an asymmetry that has been attributed to the higher salience and lower context-dependence of colour \citep{tarenskeen2015overspecification,rubio2016redundant,degen2020redundancy,tourtouri2021rational}.
Utterances with both adjectives may therefore arise mostly in size-sufficient scenes, and less often in the colour-sufficient scenes where the two accounts conflict.
Adding colour can help the listener, especially when size differences are hard to perceive \citep{rubio2016redundant,degen2020redundancy}.
This observation motivates the manipulation of size discriminability in our experiment, in which the target stands out more or less clearly as big relative to its comparison class (\Cref{sec:production-experiment}).

When speakers do produce both adjectives, previous studies show that the conventional size-first preference is so robust that, even when size is the least discriminating property, it is still preferred first, and reversed orders such as \emph{the blue big sticker} are rare \citep{danks1972prenominal,pechmann1989incremental,schlotterbeck2023incremental}.
Speakers thus keep a stable order while adapting the content of an utterance to the scene.
How the two are combined may depend on how far ahead an utterance is evaluated during production.
This scope of evaluation also determines which efficiency benefit can shape the utterance, because the compositional benefit depends on the complete phrase and the incremental benefit on each adjective.
Again, in a colour-sufficient scene, a speaker who has planned the complete utterance can begin with \emph{big}, even though \emph{big} is not the most salient adjective at that point, whereas a speaker who evaluates one adjective at a time is likely to begin with \emph{blue}.
Which order a speaker produces may thus depend on how much of the utterance is planned in advance.

At one end of this range, a global speaker compares complete utterances as wholes.
At the other, a fully incremental speaker evaluates each next adjective against the alternatives available after the words already produced \citep{cohngordon2019incremental,waldon2021modeling}.
Research on bounded planning suggests that human speakers lie between these endpoints, beginning to speak before every part of an utterance has been prepared and continuing to plan during articulation \citep{ferreira2002incremental,konopka2014priming}.
A speaker between these endpoints may choose each adjective for its immediate usefulness while looking ahead to the complete utterance, so that both pressures can influence adjective order.
We call this intermediate hypothesis \emph{plan-guided production}.
A free parameter determines the degree of planning between these endpoints.
Planning an utterance involves selecting properties for mention as well as arranging them \citep{fukumura2010use,van2019conceptualization}.
Selection and ordering together make the full distribution of one-, two-, and three-adjective utterances the relevant production outcome.

\label{sec:bg:incremental}
The Rational Speech Act (RSA) framework can predict this distribution by relating speaker choice to an utterance's informativeness and its production cost \citep{frank2012predicting,goodman2016pragmatic,degen2023rational}.
Because both efficiency accounts concern how informative an utterance is, they can be formalised within the same RSA model and compared on the same data.
The framework also assigns a probability to every possible utterance, so it extends naturally from two fixed orders to the full set of utterances that speakers produce.
\citet{schlotterbeck2023incremental} asked participants to rate size-first and colour-first utterances on a slider.
Such ratings present both orders for comparison, so they cannot show which properties speakers would mention.
Size-first preferences were strongest when size alone identified the target and weakest when colour alone did.
A direct replication of this slider study showed the same pattern (\Cref{sec:exp:slider-replication}).
They also proposed an incremental RSA model that combined the two efficiency accounts, but the model compared only two fixed adjective orders.
The separate predictive contributions of the two accounts remained unresolved.
It also remained open how they apply when speakers produce the utterances themselves.

Our central research question is: \emph{How do speakers reconcile the pressures from hierarchical semantic composition and incremental pragmatics when selecting and ordering adjectives?}
We address this question with two sets of matched model comparisons, each isolating one theoretical assumption while holding all other components constant.
The first targets the scope of evaluation that separates the two accounts.
It asks which degree of planning best predicts the utterances speakers produce.
The second asks whether narrowing the comparison class for size, as proposed by the hierarchical compositional account, improves prediction.
We test these models on a German production experiment, in which speakers described a target object in a visual scene.
Their utterances show which content, length, and order speakers produce when they formulate an utterance themselves.
Methodologically, we extend RSA production models from two fixed orders to the full set of utterances with one, two, or three adjectives for size, colour, and form.
We estimate the models hierarchically across participants and compare them by how well they predict held-out data \citep{vehtari2017practical}.
A controlled simulation then examines under which conditions hierarchical composition can favour size-first order.

\Cref{sec:task} presents the production experiment, followed by the models and their degrees of planning in \Cref{sec:model}.
\Cref{sec:modelcomparison} presents the predictive model comparisons; \Cref{sec:simulation} examines the conditions under which communication favours size-first order.
\Cref{sec:discussion} discusses the implications for the two efficiency accounts and for bounded planning in production.

\section{Production Experiment}
\label{sec:task}
\label{sec:production-experiment}

Whether speakers resolve the conflict between the two accounts by evaluating complete utterances or single adjectives becomes visible only when they select the adjectives themselves.
The experiment builds on the slider-rating study of \citet{schlotterbeck2023incremental}, in which participants compared two phrases that differed only in the order of the size and colour adjectives, such as \emph{den gro{\ss}en blauen Aufkleber} and \emph{den blauen gro{\ss}en Aufkleber}.
A direct replication of the slider study showed the same relation between referential context and adjective-order preference: size-first ratings were strongest when size alone identified the target and weakest when colour alone did, and a small size-first preference remained in colour-sufficient displays (\Cref{sec:exp:slider-replication}).
The interaction between size discriminability and referential context observed in the original data was not clearly replicated.
The present experiment used the same displays and manipulations, but replaced the comparison of the two phrases with direct production.
Direct production allows adjective selection and utterance length to vary along with order.
As in the slider study, the experiment manipulated referential context within subjects and size discriminability between subjects.
Referential context varies whether size, colour, or both are needed to identify the target, manipulating the relative usefulness of the two properties \citep{fukumura2018ordering,rubio2020incrementality,rubio2021wordorder}.
Size discriminability separately varies how clearly size differences can be perceived.
Crossing these factors allows us to ask whether the influence of size discriminability depends on which properties support reference, and how these pressures affect adjective selection, length, and order.


\subsection{Method}

\subsubsection{Participants}

The experiment was conducted online via Prolific, with compensation set slightly above the platform's median level.
Participants reported German as their native language.
The retained sample comprised 113 participants, 60 in the low- and 53 in the high-discriminability group.
Participants from the slider studies were ineligible.
Exclusion criteria and the trials retained for each analysis appear in \Cref{tab:exclusion-transparency}.

\subsubsection{Materials}

The task was presented as a reference game about collectible stickers: a partner saw the same stickers in a different order and lacked the target frame, making spatial descriptions uninformative.
On each critical trial, participants saw six objects with one framed target (\Cref{fig:design}, Panel A).
The noun was always \emph{Aufkleber} (`sticker'), and the materials varied the objects' size, colour, and form across counterbalanced lists.
The target was always the largest object of its colour and form, so it could always be described as \emph{big}.
Stimulus construction and trial counts appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:methods-transparency}.

\subsubsection{Design}

The experiment used a 3 (referential context: first property sufficient, second property sufficient, both necessary; within-subjects) $\times$ 2 (size discriminability: high, low; between-subjects) mixed design.
Each context was realised with three adjective pairs (size--colour, size--form, colour--form), which determined the two properties that could distinguish the target.
Referential context changed which properties were needed to identify the target; in size--colour trials, for example, size alone (size sufficient), colour alone (colour sufficient), or both properties (both necessary) identified the target.
Target--competitor size differences were more pronounced in the high-discriminability group than in the low-discriminability group (\Cref{fig:design}).
The behavioural analyses focus on size--colour trials, the classic contrast in the ordering literature and our motivating example.
The production models are fitted to all three adjective pairs, with colour--form trials providing a baseline without a subjective adjective.
The two experimental hypotheses concern the interaction between the two factors.
Lower size discriminability should increase redundant adjective use more strongly when size alone is sufficient, and higher discriminability should increase size-initial responses more strongly when both properties are necessary.

\subsubsection{Procedure}

Participants saw the framed target together with the fixed sentence frame \emph{den \_\_\_ Aufkleber} and typed the adjective material before the noun.
This response format allowed us to observe which properties they mentioned, how many adjectives they used, and how they ordered them.
Practice trials introduced the relevant colour and form vocabulary, and main-trial order was randomised within blocks.

\subsubsection{Response Coding and Analysis}

Responses were annotated following guidelines that preserve adjective content and order.
A production response could contain a single adjective, such as \emph{blue}; two adjectives, such as \emph{big blue}; or three, such as \emph{big blue round}.
Coding each adjective as size (\adj{S}), colour (\adj{C}), or form (\adj{F}) gives fifteen ordered categories: three single adjectives, six two-adjective sequences, and six three-adjective sequences.
All production models assign probabilities to these fifteen categories.
The complete annotation scheme, including the treatment of responses outside these categories, appears in \Cref{sec:appendix:methods-transparency}.

The annotated responses were further classified for the two experimental hypotheses.
\emph{Redundant adjective use} indicates a response containing more properties than needed for unique reference: multiple adjectives in a one-property-sufficient context, or three adjectives when both properties are needed.
\emph{Size-initiality} indicates that a response begins with size, including a response consisting of size alone.
It reflects adjective selection as well as order.
Order conditional on producing both size and colour is described separately.
The two outcomes were analysed with Bayesian hierarchical models containing referential context, size discriminability, and their interaction; the full specifications and planned contrasts appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:methods-transparency}.

\subsection{Results}

\paragraph{Adjective content and length.}
The observed response distribution indicated that referential context shaped adjective content and utterance length (\Cref{fig:production-responses}, Panel A).
Both-necessary scenes generally elicited longer utterances, whereas one-property-sufficient scenes more often elicited a single adjective.
Colour-sufficient scenes were commonly resolved with colour alone, so no size--colour ordering decision was observed on those trials.
Production also revealed substantial use of three-adjective utterances.

\paragraph{Redundant adjective use.}
When size alone identified the target, most responses still contained more than one adjective (\Cref{fig:production-responses}, Panel B).
Such redundant utterances were more frequent when size differences were hard to see, by $23.4$ percentage points under low discriminability ($95\%$ CrI $[15.1,31.8]$; posterior probability $>.999$).
In both-necessary and colour-sufficient displays, discriminability made a smaller difference, and the credible intervals included zero.
The Bayesian hierarchical analysis confirmed that the effect of discriminability was larger when size alone was sufficient than on average in the other two contexts (difference $=15.3$ percentage points, $95\%$ CrI $[7.2,23.6]$; posterior probability $>.999$ in the hypothesised direction).

\paragraph{Initial adjective and conditional order.}
Speakers began with size most often when size alone identified the target, less often when both properties were needed, and least often when colour alone did (\Cref{fig:production-responses}, Panel C).
Both differences were credible ($19.3$ percentage points, $95\%$ CrI $[13.9,24.8]$; $53.1$ percentage points, $95\%$ CrI $[45.9,60.1]$).
Size discriminability mattered mainly when both properties were needed (difference from the one-property-sufficient contexts $=16.9$ percentage points, $95\%$ CrI $[7.7,25.6]$; posterior probability $>.999$ in the hypothesised direction).
In these displays, speakers began with size more often when size differences were easy to see ($14.1$ percentage points, $95\%$ CrI $[3.6,24.2]$; posterior probability $.997$).
In the two one-property-sufficient contexts, discriminability had at most a small effect on size-initial responses.
Conditional on producing both size and colour, size-first utterances remained more frequent, and colour-first utterances were comparatively rare.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:production-discussion}

The results supported the hypothesis that reduced size discriminability particularly increases redundant adjective use when size alone can identify the target.
They also supported the hypothesis that higher size discriminability particularly favours size-initial responses when both properties are needed to identify the target.
Speakers were more likely to begin with size when it was more useful for reference, including when they used size as the only adjective.
The conditional size-first preference describes responses containing both properties, while size-initiality also includes the decision to use size alone.
Referential context also affected how often speakers produced the two adjectives whose order the two efficiency accounts compare.

Three-adjective responses extend beyond the material required for unique reference and involve the selection and ordering of several adjectives.
Modelling these data requires assigning probability to utterances of different lengths and contents, including redundant utterances.
The following framework represents these alternatives within a common utterance space, allowing us to compare how hierarchical composition and the degree of planning contribute to their use.

\section{A Plan-Guided Production Model}
\label{sec:model}

In the colour-sufficient scene of the introduction, a fully planned speaker compares complete utterances, such as \emph{blue}, \emph{big blue}, and \emph{blue big}, whereas a fully incremental speaker evaluates the first adjective on its own.
Both speakers pursue the same goal of identifying the target; they differ only in how far ahead they look.
Which of them do human speakers resemble, or do they lie in between?

The Rational Speech Act (RSA) framework models a speaker who selects utterances according to their informativeness about the intended referent and their production cost \citep{frank2012predicting,goodman2016pragmatic}.
Standard RSA speakers evaluate complete utterances, whereas incremental variants evaluate one word at a time \citep{cohngordon2019incremental}.
Our model keeps this measure of informativeness but treats the choice between these two forms of evaluation as a matter of degree, estimated from the data.
Because planning cannot be observed directly in typed utterances, the model infers it from the distribution of utterances that speakers produce: which properties they mention, how many adjectives they use, and how they order them.
The models also differ in whether the comparison class for size is updated as adjective meanings compose from the noun outwards.
Panel B of \Cref{fig:overview} shows these two dimensions.


\subsection{Literal Interpretation and Meaning Function}
\label{sec:model:response-space}
\label{sec:model:semantic-comparison}

All models use the observed six-object display.
Size has a graded meaning: an object counts as \emph{big} to the degree that it is larger than the other objects in its comparison class \citep{schmidt2009how,franke2019subjectivity}.
Colour meanings are graded as well: objects of the named colour receive most, but not all, of the weight.
Form is treated as uninformative in the literal semantics; speakers' use of form is captured by salience and by the length and selection terms introduced below.
These semantic settings are shared across the production architectures.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
For every complete or partial adjective string, the literal interpretation $L$ composes the adjectives hierarchically, applying modification from the noun outwards.
$L$ corresponds to the literal listener of RSA models and serves here as a semantic layer rather than a separate agent.
For example, in \emph{big blue sticker}, \emph{blue} modifies \emph{sticker} before \emph{big} is interpreted.
Let $c_1,\ldots,c_T$ denote the modifiers of a string in this order, so that $c_1$ is the adjective next to the noun.
Starting from a uniform prior over the objects, $C_0=P_0$, composition updates a distribution over objects at each step:
\begin{equation}
  C_j(o)
  \propto
  C_{j-1}(o)\,
  \llbracket c_j\rrbracket_{\rho_j}(o),
  \qquad j=1,\ldots,T,
  \label{eq:hierarchical-composition}
\end{equation}
where $\llbracket c_j\rrbracket_{\rho_j}$ is the modifier's meaning under the selected semantic regime.
The final state gives the literal interpretation of the string as a distribution over objects.

Hierarchical composition affects order only if an inner adjective can change how an outer adjective is interpreted.
The two semantic regimes differ in exactly this respect.
Under \emph{context-fixed} semantics, the comparison class for size remains the whole scene.
Under \emph{sequential context updating}, the comparison class is recomputed after each modifier, so that in \emph{big blue}, \emph{big} is judged among objects weighted by how well they match \emph{blue}.
Because colour meanings are graded, this restriction is partial.
When it makes the target more distinctive in size, updating can make size-first order more informative.
The threshold construction and semantic parameters appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:model-specification,sec:appendix:semantic-details}.

Interpretation proceeds from the noun outwards, but production proceeds from left to right.
The speaker evaluates the information available after each adjective in the surface sequence.
Let $u_{\leq t}=w_1\ldots w_t$ be the prefix comprising the first $t$ adjectives, and $P_0(o\mid X)=1/6$ the object prior in display $X$.
Each prefix is interpreted from this prior, applying each of its modifiers once in noun-outward order.
For \emph{big blue}, the first prefix is \emph{big} alone, and the second is the composed meaning of \emph{big blue}.
Under context-fixed semantics, the interpretation of a prefix is
\begin{equation}
  L(o\mid u_{\leq t},X)
  \propto
  P_0(o\mid X)\prod_{j=1}^{t}
  \llbracket w_j\rrbracket_{\mathrm{fix}}(o).
  \label{eq:prefix-listener}
\end{equation}
Under updating, the comparison class for \emph{big} changes as the prefix grows from \emph{big} to \emph{big blue}.
The probability that this interpretation assigns to the target is the communicative value of the prefix.

\subsection{Production Architectures}
\label{sec:model:architectures}

The three production architectures share a \emph{prefix utility}, which accumulates how well the adjectives in an utterance identify the target at each adjective position.
This accumulation means that an utterance gains value when it identifies the target early, not only when it is complete.
The production architecture determines how this accumulated utility is compared with alternatives: across complete utterances, or among the adjectives available at each point.
At position $t$, the utility of candidate adjective $a$ for target $o^*$ is
\begin{equation}
  U_t(a\mid u_{<t},o^*,X)
  = \alpha\log L(o^*\mid u_{<t}+a,X)
  + \lambda_{\mathrm{salience}}z_a,
  \label{eq:prefix-raw-utility}
\end{equation}
where the pragmatic-optimality parameter $\alpha$ controls sensitivity to informativeness and $z_a$ represents the visual salience of the candidate property.
The two terms capture what can make an adjective attractive as soon as it is produced: it discriminates the target, or it is visually salient.
For a completed utterance $u$, the model combines the accumulated utility of its selected adjectives with a term for competition among alternatives at each adjective position:
\begin{align}
  U^{\mathrm{complete}}(u,o^*,X)
  &=\sum_t U_t(w_t\mid u_{<t},o^*,X),\\
  \mathcal N^{\mathrm{local}}(u,o^*,X)
  &=\sum_t\log\!\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(u_{<t})}
    \exp U_t(a\mid u_{<t},o^*,X),
  \label{eq:path-components}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal A(u_{<t})$ contains the adjective types still available after the current prefix.
The complete-utterance utility $U^{\mathrm{complete}}$ is the sum of the utilities at each adjective position and is shared by all three production architectures.
The local-normalisation term $\mathcal N^{\mathrm{local}}$ records the utility of the competing adjectives at each position.
When adjectives compete at a given point, a selected adjective receives lower probability if other available adjectives are also useful there.
For example, \emph{big blue} and \emph{blue big} contain the same properties but face different competitors at the first position.
When colour alone identifies the target, \emph{blue} is a strong competitor, so \emph{big} is less likely to come first.
Two completed utterances with similar $U^{\mathrm{complete}}$ can receive different probabilities because they pass through prefixes with different degrees of competition.
These two terms correspond to the two influences in plan-guided production: the value of the complete utterance and the competition among adjectives at each point.

The probability of a completed utterance is
\begin{equation}
  S_{\kappa}(u\mid o^*,X)
  \propto
  \exp\!\left\{
    U^{\mathrm{complete}}(u,o^*,X)
    -\kappa\mathcal N^{\mathrm{local}}(u,o^*,X)
    +\beta\Omega(u)
    +T(u,X)
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:plan-guided-speaker}
\end{equation}
The parameter $\kappa$ determines how strongly competition among the adjectives available at each point contributes to the probability of a completed utterance.
With $\kappa=0$, this competition drops out, and the \emph{global} speaker compares only the fifteen complete utterances.
With $\kappa=1$, the \emph{fully incremental} speaker produces each adjective by comparing it with the adjectives available at that point, without looking ahead; the probability of an utterance then combines these local choices with the shared terms below.\footnote{This contrast corresponds to that between locally and globally normalised sequence models in natural language processing \citep{lafferty2001conditional,andor2016globally}.}
Intermediate values define the \emph{plan-guided} speaker, in which both comparisons contribute.
Lower values of $\kappa$ therefore correspond to a greater degree of planning.
In the colour-sufficient scene, $\kappa$ thus governs whether a speaker is drawn to \emph{blue}, the most useful adjective at that point, or can still begin with \emph{big} as part of a planned utterance.
Even the global speaker still evaluates each prefix; it differs only in comparing complete utterances.

Besides informativeness and salience, the utterances are shaped by tendencies that are not specific to planning: speakers follow conventional orders, avoid unnecessarily long utterances, prefer a single adjective when it suffices, and add properties when size is hard to see.
The slider replication, for example, showed a small size-first preference even when colour alone identified the target (\Cref{sec:exp:slider-replication}).
Our account does not aim to derive these tendencies, but a model that ignored them would misattribute their effects to the degree of planning.
Following common practice in Bayesian cognitive modelling, we include them as auxiliary components that link the model to the task \citep{lee2013bayesian,franke2016task}: a fixed order score $\Omega(u)$ from a German language model, weighted by $\beta$, and length and selection terms $T(u,X)$.
The order score reflects conventions learned from language use, which may themselves have been shaped by efficiency pressures over time \citep{franke2019subjectivity}.
Their parameters are estimated jointly with $\kappa$, so uncertainty about them is carried into the inference about planning.
Because these components are identical across architectures, the architectures differ only in how utterances are evaluated.
Their specification appears in \Cref{sec:appendix:model-specification}.

\section{Model Comparison and Predictive Evidence}
\label{sec:experiments}
\label{sec:modelcomparison}
\label{sec:model:extended}

The model comparison tests how hierarchical semantic composition and the degree of planning contribute to the observed distribution of utterances.
Crossing the three production architectures with the two semantic regimes gives six matched models (\Cref{fig:overview}, Panel B), which share all other components.
Two further models refine the plan-guided architecture with participant-specific $\kappa_i$ under each semantic regime.
All also allow participant variation in pragmatic optimality $\alpha_i$ and stable-order weight $\beta_i$.
The comparison asks whether an intermediate degree of planning improves prediction over both endpoints, whether updating the size comparison class adds further predictive value, and whether allowing the degree of planning to vary across speakers improves prediction.
We then examine which production patterns the best-predicting model captures.

\subsection{Inference and Predictive Comparison}
\label{sec:model:inference}

We fitted the production models to the 9{,}100 annotated responses from the 113 participants in the production experiment, including size--colour, size--form, and colour--form trials.
Each response was represented as one of the fifteen ordered adjective categories used by every model.
Bayesian inference was performed in NumPyro \citep{phan2019composable}.

Approximate leave-one-out expected log predictive density (ELPD) measures how much probability each model assigns to held-out responses \citep{vehtari2017practical}.
Higher ELPD indicates better prediction of the complete utterance distribution.
Uncertainty in paired model comparisons is estimated with a participant-level Bayesian bootstrap and reported as 95\% credible intervals.
As a robustness check, a ten-fold cross-validation over participants predicted each held-out participant as a new speaker and led to the same conclusions for the architecture, semantic, and participant-variation comparisons reported below (\Cref{sec:appendix:robustness}).
In total, the comparisons rest on 66 fitted production models: the eight models above, 50 cross-validation fits, and eight refits for the robustness checks in \Cref{sec:appendix:robustness}.
The inference procedure and numerical diagnostics appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:model-diagnostics}.

\subsection{Production Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture-comparison}

For the question of which degree of planning best predicts production, the Bayesian model comparison favoured plan-guided production over both global and fully incremental production (\Cref{fig:production-architecture-results}, Panel A).
Averaged across semantic regimes, the plan-guided model had higher predictive accuracy than the global model ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=256.9$, 95\% CrI $[71.8,404.7]$) and the fully incremental model ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=548.5$, 95\% CrI $[349.8,795.1]$).
These contrasts supported the plan-guided production hypothesis that the value of the complete utterance and local adjective competition jointly shape the response distribution.
Both contrasts held when the two condition-specific length and selection terms were removed from all models (\Cref{sec:appendix:robustness}).
In the context-fixed model, the posterior mean of the shared $\kappa$ was $.446$, with a 95\% CrI of $[.407,.486]$ (Panel B).
The estimate lay between the global and fully incremental endpoints.

\label{sec:architecture-residuals}
Under context-fixed semantics, the plan-guided advantage was credible in all three adjective families and largest in size--colour trials ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=202.4$, 95\% CrI $[169.3,237.2]$; \Cref{sec:appendix:robustness}).
Colour-initial responses accounted for $81.7\%$ of the plan-guided model's ELPD advantage over the average of the global and fully incremental models.
In the model, for example, once \emph{blue} has been produced, size and form compete for the next position, and this competition differs from that faced by an utterance beginning with \emph{round}.
Different opening adjectives leave different continuations available, allowing competition at later positions to affect the probabilities of the completed utterances.

\subsection{Plan-Guided Production and Participant Variation}
\label{sec:participant-variation}

Allowing $\kappa$ to vary across participants yielded a credible improvement over the otherwise matched model with a shared $\kappa$.
Under context-fixed semantics, the participant-specific model gained $364.7$ ELPD (95\% CrI $[248.2,521.0]$); the gain under sequential context updating was similar.
The posterior mean population location remained intermediate between the global and fully incremental endpoints ($\mu_{\kappa}=.472$, 95\% CrI $[.337,.618]$), alongside substantial variation in participant-specific $\kappa_i$.
The participant-specific model also predicted better than a benchmark that assigns each participant the better-predicting of the two endpoint models ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=249.7$, 95\% CrI $[103.8,416.1]$), so its gain does not reduce to a mixture of global-like and fully incremental participants (\Cref{sec:appendix:robustness}).

The predictive benefit was concentrated in two- and three-adjective utterances, whereas predictions of single-adjective responses became less accurate.
The gains for longer utterances outweighed this loss and extended to three-adjective utterances, which contain redundant information in the present task.
\Cref{sec:appendix:participant-audit} reports the response-length contrasts and participant-level diagnostics.
\label{sec:production-scope}

\subsection{Hierarchical Semantic Composition}
We next asked whether narrowing the comparison class for size, as proposed by the hierarchical compositional account, improves prediction.
The comparison favoured context-fixed semantics over sequential context updating under all three production architectures (\Cref{fig:production-architecture-results}, Panel C).
The average updating-minus-fixed contrast was negative ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=-43.5$, 95\% CrI $[-56.0,-31.8]$), indicating a credible predictive advantage for the context-fixed models.
This advantage remained in the participant-specific plan-guided comparison.
Context-fixed semantics also predicted better when colour meanings were made more reliable (updating minus fixed $\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=-45.9$, 95\% CrI $[-59.9,-32.0]$), although both semantic regimes then predicted worse overall (\Cref{sec:appendix:robustness}).

Decomposing the participant-specific model comparison showed that the semantic difference chiefly concerned how much speakers said and which properties they mentioned.
Context-fixed semantics improved predictions of utterance length ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=21.5$, 95\% CrI $[12.4,30.9]$) and adjective content conditional on length ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=31.1$, 95\% CrI $[23.5,39.2]$).
For order conditional on adjective content, the estimated advantage was small and its interval included zero ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=1.5$, 95\% CrI $[-1.8,4.9]$).

Full contrasts and the score decomposition appear in \Cref{sec:semantic-score-decomposition}.

The predictive evidence thus locates the resolution of the conflict in how far ahead utterances are evaluated.
Plan-guided production predicted best, whereas narrowing the comparison class for size did not improve prediction.
The predictive comparisons do not show whether either semantic regime favours size-first order communicatively.
The following simulation examines when such a preference arises and how it changes when adjective selection and utterance length enter the speaker's alternatives.

\section{Simulation: Conditions for a Size-First Preference}
\label{sec:simulation}

A communicative account of adjective ordering seeks to explain why one order can be more useful for identifying a referent than another.
Earlier simulations found that, on average, reference favours orders that place more subjective adjectives farther from the noun, motivating a pressure that could gradually stabilise ordering preferences across repeated language use \citep{franke2019subjectivity}.
Sequential context updating provides a mechanism for this preference: the noun-adjacent adjective restricts the context in which an outer, more subjective adjective is interpreted.
The production findings place this proposal within a broader set of choices, because speakers also decide which adjectives to include and how much to say.
We therefore ask how the ordering preference changes when the speaker's utility includes information at each adjective position and the speaker can produce utterances of different lengths.

\subsection{Controlled Changes in Utility and Available Utterances}
\label{sec:simulation:boundary}

The simulation reuses the same 24{,}000 randomly generated displays across all comparisons.
Displays vary in object count and size distribution; the largest object is the target and is assigned blue.
Varying the number of blue distractors changes how strongly colour restricts the comparison class for size.
The scene-generation procedure and semantic parameter grid appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:sim-params}.

We introduce the production model's assumptions in controlled steps (\Cref{fig:simulation-controlled}, Panel A).
The first comparison gives a global speaker two alternatives, \emph{big blue} and \emph{blue big}, scored by the literal target probability after the completed utterance.
The second retains global production ($\kappa=0$) but introduces the prefix utility in \Cref{sec:model:architectures}, summing literal log probabilities of the target at each adjective position.
For \emph{big blue}, the utility then includes the information supplied by \emph{big} as well as by the completed utterance.
Holding global production fixed isolates the contribution of this prefix information.
We next add the single-adjective alternatives \emph{big} and \emph{blue}, and finally expand to the fifteen utterances used in production.
These four comparisons average over the same grid of 125 semantic parameter settings under each semantic regime.

A further comparison holds the fifteen utterances and production semantic constants fixed while varying the contribution of local competition: global ($\kappa=0$), plan-guided ($\kappa=.5$), and fully incremental ($\kappa=1$) production (Panel B).
All three architectures use the prefix utility.
Throughout these comparisons, pragmatic optimality is $\alpha=1$, order, salience, length, and selection terms are zero, and final Laplace smoothing is omitted.
\Cref{sec:appendix:controlled-simulation} gives the full specification.

The pragmatic listener $L^{\mathrm{prag}}$ applies Bayes' rule to the speaker distribution, using an equal prior over objects.
An object receives more probability when a speaker intending that object would be more likely to produce the observed utterance.
For each context, the size-first preference compares the listener's target probability after the two orders:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{SF}}
  =L^{\mathrm{prag}}(o^*\mid\text{``big blue''})
  -L^{\mathrm{prag}}(o^*\mid\text{``blue big''}).
  \label{eq:simulation-advantage}
\end{equation}
Positive values favour size-first order.
We call this measure the listener-based size-first preference, to distinguish it from the order preferences that speakers showed in production.
Other available utterances can affect this measure because the listener reasons about the speaker's choice among them.

\subsection{Conditions under which an Order Asymmetry Emerges}
\label{sec:simulation:conditions}

With global production and the terminal utility, sequential context updating generated a mean size-first preference of approximately $1.1$ percentage points, while context-fixed semantics yielded no difference between the orders (\Cref{fig:simulation-controlled}, Panel A).
With fixed meanings, the two adjective orders have the same completed interpretation and therefore the same target probability under this utility.
Updating breaks this symmetry because colour restricts the comparison class for size.
This comparison recovers the proposed communicative contribution of hierarchical composition.

Including information available at each adjective position changed the result.
With only the two orders available, the global production model yielded a mean colour-first preference under both semantic regimes: approximately $2.7$ percentage points with fixed meanings and $1.8$ with updating.
The first adjectives can differ in informativeness even when the completed meanings coincide, allowing an ordering asymmetry under fixed semantics.
Its direction, however, depends on the alternatives available for reference.
Adding the single-adjective responses changed the mean preference to size-first under both regimes, to approximately $2.3$ and $2.8$ percentage points, respectively.
The speaker can now identify a referent with one adjective, changing how probability is distributed over the two-adjective utterances and how the pragmatic listener interprets their use.
Adjective selection and utterance length thus affect the communicative value of an order even when the two orders themselves remain unchanged.

Expanding to fifteen utterances retained the mean size-first preference, at approximately $2.6$ percentage points with fixed semantics and $3.1$ with updating.
At the semantic constants used in production, all three production architectures generated a mean size-first preference under both semantic regimes (\Cref{fig:simulation-controlled}, Panel B).
The preference was approximately $1.9$ percentage points for global production, $1.5$ for plan-guided production, and $1.0$ for fully incremental production, with nearly identical values for fixed and updating semantics within each architecture.
Increasing the contribution of local adjective competition reduced the magnitude of the preference while preserving its direction.

The simulation shows how the interpretation and selection of adjectives determine which order communication favours.
Updating can favour size-first order between two complete utterances, but once single adjectives are available, the option of a shorter utterance changes how the listener interprets the same two-adjective orders.

\section{General Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The present findings connect adjective ordering to the cognitive organisation of reference production.
Stable size-first preferences coexist with context-sensitive selection of descriptive content and length.
Among the matched production models, the best predictions combine evaluation of complete utterances with continued influence from local adjective competition.
Participant-specific $\kappa$ further improves prediction, particularly for utterances containing several adjectives.
These findings motivate a plan-guided account of production in which speakers anticipate the communicative value of a complete utterance.
For the question of how speakers reconcile hierarchical composition and incremental pragmatics, the results point to the scope of evaluation rather than to the interpretation of size.
In the plan-guided model, the two efficiency pressures mainly shaped which adjectives were mentioned and which adjective came first, whereas the order of size and colour, once both were mentioned, largely followed a stable convention.
In the scenes where the two accounts conflict, speakers mostly produced colour alone, so that no ordering of size and colour was observed; when they mentioned both properties, they still placed size first.
In the plan-guided model, both patterns are consistent with local competition favouring a colour-only utterance and the stable order term maintaining size-first order in longer utterances.

\subsection{Bounded Planning and Redundant Utterances}

A useful utterance must be selected from alternatives that differ in content, length, and order.
As the number of available properties increases, their combinations and orderings grow combinatorially, making exhaustive comparison increasingly demanding.
The cognitive problem is how speakers preserve sensitivity to the communicative consequences of an utterance while limiting the alternatives and continuations they evaluate.
Computational tractability constrains the processes and representations that can support this behaviour \citep{vanrooij2008tractable}; approximation alone provides no general guarantee of tractability \citep{kwisthout2011bayesian}.

Plan-guided production motivates a hypothesis about how planning could be organised under these constraints.
An utterance-level plan could specify properties relevant to identifying the target, constraining the alternatives considered as each adjective is produced.
Local choices could remain sensitive to the developing utterance and the properties still available for mention.
This organisation accords with evidence that conceptual planning and linguistic formulation can proceed with different scopes \citep{griffin2000eyes,ferreira2002incremental,konopka2014priming}.
It also connects to good-enough processing, in which representations can remain partial while serving the current task \citep{ferreira2002goodenough}.

Redundant utterances provide a case in which planning and communicative goals interact.
A property may be selected because it becomes accessible early, because it helps the listener identify the target, or because both pressures favour its inclusion \citep{pechmann1989incremental,engelhardt2006speakers,rubio2016redundant,degen2020redundancy}.
Readily available information can guide visual search or hedge uncertainty about another property \citep{rubio2019overinformative,tourtouri2021rational}.
The improvement from participant-specific $\kappa$, concentrated in longer utterances, suggests that the balance between evaluation of the complete utterance and local competition varies across speakers.

The present model specifies a distribution over completed utterances and evaluates the full response space.
A process account must additionally explain how speakers generate candidate utterances and allocate computation to evaluating them.
Manipulating planning time, and recording speech onset and eye movements, could test whether information in later adjectives affects which adjective speakers produce first.

\subsection{Semantic Stability in Reference Production}

The context-fixed models predicted production more accurately than models that recomputed the size comparison class during composition, as proposed by the compositional account.
For the participant-specific models, this advantage chiefly concerned adjective content and utterance length; the difference in order conditional on content was small and uncertain.
The result makes the maintenance of a scene-based size comparison a plausible component of reference production in this task.
A speaker could establish which objects count as large or small during scene apprehension and retain that comparison while selecting adjectives.

The full scene and marked target provide information from which this comparison can be formed before production begins.
In discourse, a subset of referents may become relevant through a preceding utterance, potentially changing the context against which size is evaluated.
Varying when contextual information becomes available would help identify when speakers retain an initial comparison and when they revise it.
Independently varying lexical subjectivity and visual discriminability would further distinguish uncertainty about a property's interpretation from difficulty perceiving it in a particular scene.

\subsection{Available Alternatives and Communicative Efficiency}

The simulation returns to the case that motivated the study, in which \emph{blue} alone identifies the target but speakers who mention both properties still say \emph{big blue}.
The simulation shows that the availability of shorter utterances changes which of the same two adjective orders communication favours.
The value of an order depends on which other utterances the speaker can produce and which alternatives the listener expects the speaker to have considered.
This dependence gives a computational basis for studying ordering within the full distribution of content and length.

An account of reference production must consequently explain how alternatives become available.
An utterance-level plan could direct consideration towards properties relevant to the referential goal, while local competition influences which property is mentioned next.

The present data come from German, in which adjectives precede the noun.
In languages with postnominal adjectives, the noun-adjacent adjective is also the first adjective the listener hears.
Subjectivity-based ordering preferences have been reported for the mirror-image order in such a language \citep{trainin2021s}.
Incremental pragmatics, by contrast, still favours placing the more discriminating adjective first.
The two accounts therefore agree when the noun-adjacent property is also the more discriminating one, and conflict when it is not.
A postnominal version of the simulation, in which composition follows the surface order, shows that updating then weakens rather than strengthens the listener-based size-first preference (\Cref{sec:appendix:postnominal}).
Word order also affects which properties speakers mention.
\citet{rubio2021wordorder} found that English speakers produced more redundant colour adjectives than Spanish speakers, for whom colour follows the noun.
\citet{waldon2021modeling} captured this difference with an incremental RSA production model.
Listeners of prenominal and postnominal languages also integrate visual context in different orders \citep{rubio2020incrementality}.
Postnominal languages provide a test of plan-guided production in which the scenes that create a conflict between the two accounts differ from those in German.
Freer production with new participants and materials would test how the plan-guided account extends beyond these German prenominal utterances.

The broader contribution is an account of adjective ordering embedded in the construction of a useful referential utterance.
In the model, scene-based meanings, stable order expectations, and local adjective competition determine which properties are mentioned and how they are arranged.
The plan-guided framework connects these choices to the evaluation of a complete utterance, while the simulation shows how available alternatives affect the interpretation of an utterance.
Together, these findings motivate an account of boundedly rational communication in which partial planning supports good-enough production.

\subsection{Design and Hypotheses}

The studies shared the reference-game setting and display design described in \Cref{sec:task}.
For slider judgements, greater referential usefulness of size predicts stronger size-first preference, yielding the ordering size-sufficient $>$ both-necessary $>$ colour-sufficient; a stable size-first preference predicts ratings above neutrality even when colour alone identifies the target.
The slider interaction contrasts assess how discriminability effects differ across contexts, with the replication testing whether the pattern in the original data recurs.

\subsection{Procedure}

Each study began with three practice trials with feedback.
Main trials were divided into four blocks of approximately equal length, with self-paced breaks and independently randomised trial order within each block.
In the original slider study, participants completed 81 critical trials and 99 filler or control trials (180 total), with a mean completion time of 27.6 minutes.
Control trials had an unambiguous expected response; the additional ``none fits'' option allowed participants to indicate that neither utterance identified the target.
Frequent use of this option on experimental trials was an exclusion criterion, as detailed in \Cref{tab:exclusion-transparency}.
The replication used the same 1{,}080 combinations of list, item, and condition.

On each critical trial, two phrases differing in adjective order appeared on opposite sides of a continuous slider, together with a ``none of the descriptions fits'' option.
Left--right presentation was randomised across trials.
Ratings were centred so that zero indicated neutrality, positive values favoured size-first, and negative values favoured colour-first order, on a scale from $-0.5$ to $0.5$.

Participants typed adjective material into the frame \emph{den \_\_\_ Aufkleber}.
Practice trials introduced the relevant colour and form vocabulary.
In the production experiment, participants completed 81 critical and 54 filler or control trials (135 total), with a mean completion time of 32.4 minutes.

\paragraph{Behavioural models.}
The behavioural hypotheses were evaluated with Bayesian hierarchical models.
All behavioural analyses used the size--colour subsets: 3{,}485 observations in the original slider study, 2{,}786 in the replication, and 3{,}034 in the production experiment.
For the slider analyses, ratings were reoriented toward size-first, divided by 100, and centred by subtracting $0.5$.
Let $\mathbf{x}_{ij}$ encode referential context, size discriminability, and their interaction, and let $\mathbf{z}_{ij}$ encode the participant intercept and context slopes.
The slider models used a Gaussian likelihood with
\[
  y_{ij}\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_{ij},\sigma),
  \qquad
  \mu_{ij}=\mathbf{x}_{ij}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta+\mathbf{z}_{ij}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}_{i},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{b}_{i}\sim\operatorname{MVN}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol\Sigma_{\mathrm{participant}}).
\]
For production, size-initiality indicated whether the coded response began with the size adjective.
Redundant adjective use indicated a three-adjective response in both-necessary contexts or a multi-adjective response in one-property-sufficient contexts.
Both binary outcomes used a Bernoulli likelihood with a logit link and an item intercept $c_k$:
\[
  y_{ijk}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\pi_{ijk}),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{logit}(\pi_{ijk})=\mathbf{x}_{ijk}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta+\mathbf{z}_{ijk}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}_{i}+c_k,
  \qquad
  c_k\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{item}}).
\]
For the slider studies, the interaction was represented by two posterior contrasts: the high-minus-low discriminability effect in the both-necessary context minus the corresponding effect in the size-sufficient context, and the high-minus-low effect in the colour-sufficient context minus the effect in the size-sufficient context.
The production interactions were planned contrasts on the posterior-predictive probability scale.
For size-initiality, the contrast was the high-minus-low discriminability effect in the both-necessary context minus the mean effect in the two one-property-sufficient contexts.
For redundant adjective use, the contrast was the low-minus-high discriminability effect in the size-sufficient context minus the mean effect in the both-necessary and colour-sufficient contexts.

The slider models used a Gaussian likelihood, correlated participant intercepts and context slopes, fixed-effect priors $\operatorname{Normal}(0,.30)$, $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.20)$ priors on participant standard deviations, and a $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.25)$ prior on residual standard deviation.
The production models used Bernoulli likelihoods, the same correlated participant effects, an item intercept, a $\operatorname{Normal}(0,2)$ intercept prior, $\operatorname{Normal}(0,1.5)$ priors on the remaining fixed effects, and $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(1)$ priors on participant and item standard deviations.
Participant-effect correlations received an LKJ$(2)$ prior.
Four chains run in parallel each provided $1{,}000$ retained draws after $1{,}000$ warm-up iterations.
All four fits had zero divergences, no maximum-tree-depth hits, $\widehat{R}\leq1.01$, and minimum effective sample sizes above $900$.
For each posterior draw, posterior-predictive cell expectations were computed for a new participant and, in production, a new item by integrating over the fitted random-effects distributions.
The planned contrasts were then evaluated on the rating or response-probability scale.

\subsection{Representation of Experimental Displays}

The model represents each recorded display as a $6\times3$ array of size, colour, and form values, with the intended referent indexed first.

\subsection{Graded Size Meaning}

Size is represented by a graded threshold over the comparison class $\mathcal C$ \citep{schmidt2009how,franke2019subjectivity}:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{\delta}(\mathcal C)
  = Q_{.80}(\mathcal C)
    - \delta\,\bigl(Q_{.80}(\mathcal C)-Q_{.20}(\mathcal C)\bigr),
  \label{eq:size-threshold}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{.20}$ and $Q_{.80}$ are probability-weighted size quantiles and $\delta$ controls threshold selectivity.
For an object of size $s_o$, graded membership is
\begin{equation}
 m_{\mathrm S}(o\mid\mathcal C)
 =\Phi\!\left(\frac{s_o-\theta_\delta(\mathcal C)}
 {w\sqrt{s_o^2+\theta_\delta(\mathcal C)^2+\epsilon}}\right),
 \qquad w=.6856,\quad\epsilon=10^{-8},
 \label{eq:size-membership}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.
The weighted quantiles are the first observed sizes at which cumulative comparison-class probability reaches $.20$ and $.80$.
The same width $w$ is used in both discriminability groups; the recorded sizes enter this function directly.
For colour and form, matching objects receive membership $\nu$ and nonmatching objects $1-\nu$, with $\nu_{\mathrm C}=.59$ and $\nu_{\mathrm F}=.50$.
Each prefix is interpreted from the uniform object prior, applying its modifiers once in noun-outward order.
Context updating changes the comparison-class weights used for the size quantiles as inner modifiers are applied.
\Cref{eq:prefix-listener,eq:prefix-raw-utility,eq:path-components,eq:plan-guided-speaker} define how this meaning enters the literal interpretation $L$ and the three production accounts.

\Cref{fig:production-inference-plate} separates fixed theoretical assumptions, estimated parameters, participant variation, and observed responses.
The fixed variables $A$ and $R$ index production architecture and semantic regime.
For participant $i$ on trial $t$, the model maps the display and target $X_{it}$ and the stable-order score $\Omega(u)$ to a probability $\pi_{it}(u)$ over the fifteen possible utterances, from which the observed response $y_{it}$ is drawn.
The shared coefficients govern visual salience, adjective selection, and utterance length.

The form semantics, stable-order feature, and length and selection terms were specified on the present data and are identical across all compared production architectures and semantic regimes.
The population pragmatic-optimality parameter has prior $\alpha_0\sim\operatorname{HalfNormal}(3)$, and the population log stable-order weight has prior $\mu_{\log\beta}\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,.35)$.
Participant effects are noncentred: $\alpha_i=\alpha_0\exp(\tau_{\log\alpha}z_{\alpha i})$ and $\beta_i=\exp(\mu_{\log\beta}+\tau_\beta z_{\beta i})$, with independent standard-normal $z$ values, $\tau_{\log\alpha}\sim\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.15)$, and $\tau_\beta\sim\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.5)$.
The shared plan-guided weight has prior $\kappa\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$.
In the model with participant-specific $\kappa_i$, $\mu_\kappa\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $\kappa_i=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\operatorname{logit}(\mu_\kappa)+\tau_\kappa z_{\kappa i})$, with $\tau_\kappa\sim\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.5)$ and independent $z_{\kappa i}\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)$.
The salience coefficient has a $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(1)$ prior and the salience-continuation coefficient a $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.75)$ prior.
The sufficient-single-adjective and form-use coefficients each have a $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(1.5)$ prior; the three-adjective penalty and the two size-reliability coefficients each have a $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(1)$ prior.
Threshold selectivity is fixed at $\delta=.50$; colour reliability, form reliability, and perceptual blur take the values specified above.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Visual salience.}
The salience feature combines a fixed preference across properties with their contrast in the displayed scene.
For the target $o^*$, let $s_o$, $c_o$, and $f_o$ be an object's recorded size, colour, and form, and let $h$ indicate high size discriminability.
The three contrast scores are
\begin{align}
 r_{\mathrm S}&=\frac{1+h}{2}\,
 \operatorname{logistic}\!\left(
 \frac{s_{o^*}-\max_{o\ne o^*}s_o}{\operatorname{sd}(s)+10^{-8}}
 \right),\\
 r_{\mathrm C}&=1-\frac{1}{5}\sum_{o\ne o^*}\mathbf 1\{c_o=c_{o^*}\},
 &r_{\mathrm F}&=1-\frac{1}{5}\sum_{o\ne o^*}\mathbf 1\{f_o=f_{o^*}\}.
\end{align}
Here $\operatorname{sd}(s)$ is the population standard deviation across the six displayed sizes.
The fixed baseline scores are $(b_{\mathrm S},b_{\mathrm C},b_{\mathrm F})=(0,1,.25)$.
Centring across the three properties gives
\[
 z_a=b_a+r_a-\frac13\sum_v(b_v+r_v).
\]
A property receives greater salience when it contrasts with the distractors; the size contrast is additionally scaled by discriminability.
The same feature enters prefix utility through $\lambda_{\mathrm{salience}}z_a$ and the continuation cost in the length and selection terms below.

\paragraph{Stable order expectations.}
We derived the fixed order feature from \texttt{dbmdz/german-gpt2}.
For each pairing of six colour and six form adjectives, the scoring script generated all fifteen non-empty orders using those adjectives and \emph{großer}, followed by the noun \emph{Aufkleber}, without a surrounding sentence.
It evaluated the resulting 540 strings, including repeated shorter strings, using mean token surprisal in bits.
We averaged this surprisal within each order category and normalised $2^{-\overline{s}(u)}$ across the fifteen categories to obtain the fixed weights $q(u)$.
For $\mathcal U_{A(u)}$, the set of orders expressing the same adjective set as $u$, the model uses
\begin{equation}
 \Omega(u)=\log q(u)-\frac{1}{|\mathcal U_{A(u)}|}
 \sum_{v\in\mathcal U_{A(u)}}\log q(v).
 \label{eq:order-residual}
\end{equation}
This centring preserves relative order preferences within an adjective set and gives every single-adjective response a score of zero.
The scoring script, archived token-surprisal table, aggregation code, and fixed scores and residuals are supplied with the model code.
Participant-specific $\beta_i$ determines the influence of these stable expectations when the model normalises across the complete response space.

\paragraph{Length and selection terms.}
The length and selection terms $T(u,X)$ connect the referential design to choices about which properties to mention and how far to continue an utterance.
Let $n=|u|$ and $A(u)$ denote the length and unordered adjective set of $u$.
For this term, $X$ includes the display's condition labels: $d\in\{\adj{S},\adj{C},\adj{F},\varnothing\}$ is the property that identifies the target on its own, with $d=\varnothing$ in both-necessary conditions; $c$ indicates a colour-sufficient condition; and $h=1$ indicates high size discriminability ($h=0$ for low).
Using $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ for an indicator, the production models use
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T(u,X)={}&-\rho_{\mathrm{cont}}\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\max(z_{w_t},0)
 +\lambda_{\mathrm{suff}}\mathbf{1}\{d\ne\varnothing,\ u=(d)\}\\
&+\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}(1-c)\mathbf{1}\{\adj{F}\in A(u)\}
 -\lambda_{3}\mathbf{1}\{n=3\}\\
&+\lambda_{\mathrm{S}}h\,\mathbf{1}\{d=\adj{S},\ u=(\adj{S})\}\\
&+\lambda_{\mathrm{SF}}(1-2h)\mathbf{1}\{d=\adj{S},\ A(u)=\{\adj{S},\adj{F}\}\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:task-link}
\end{equation}
The continuation cost sums the positive salience scores of adjectives followed by another adjective, using the same $z$ scores as the prefix utility in \Cref{eq:prefix-raw-utility}.
The sufficiency term favours a single adjective that identifies the target, while the form term increases the value of form-containing utterances outside colour-sufficient conditions.
The three-adjective penalty offsets the value accumulated along longer utterances.
In size-sufficient conditions, the final two terms additionally favour the single size adjective at high discriminability and favour size--form pairs at low discriminability while penalising them at high discriminability.
The pair term applies equally to both orders.
All six coefficients are nonnegative and shared across participants within each fitted model, with the priors given above.
These terms enter the completed-utterance score alongside accumulated prefix utility, local competition, and the stable-order score; normalisation over all fifteen responses turns their combined values into predictions of adjective selection, length, and order.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Response likelihood.}
Writing $g(u)$ for accumulated prefix utility, $i(u)$ for the sum of local log probabilities, and $B(u)=\beta_i\Omega(u)+T(u,X)$, the computed score before Laplace smoothing is $(1-\kappa_i)g(u)+\kappa_i i(u)+B(u)$.
Normalising these scores yields $\widetilde S_{\kappa_i}(u\mid X)$, whose endpoints obey the geometric interpolation in \Cref{eq:plan-guided-interpolation}.
A final Laplace smoothing step mixes this distribution with a uniform distribution over the fifteen responses, giving the categorical response likelihood
\begin{equation}
 p(y_{it}=u\mid X_{it})=(1-.003)\widetilde S_{\kappa_i}(u\mid X_{it})+\frac{.003}{15}.
\end{equation}
The matched global and fully incremental models fix $\kappa$ at zero and one, respectively; the matched plan-guided model estimates a shared $\kappa$; the model with participant-specific $\kappa_i$ estimates them for each participant.
All models retain participant hierarchies for pragmatic optimality and stable-order weight, with the remaining task coefficients shared across participants.

\section{Production Model Comparison and Diagnostics}
\label{sec:appendix:model-diagnostics}

\subsection{Inference and Predictive Comparison}

Each likelihood evaluation computes all fifteen completed utterances exactly, with the listener and speaker computations vectorised and compiled in JAX.
All eight production fits used four No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) chains on a GPU and 1{,}000 warm-up iterations per chain.
The two global models retained 4{,}000 draws per chain at maximum tree depth 8; the other six models retained 1{,}000 draws per chain at depth 6.
The eight fits had zero divergences, maximum $\widehat R<1.01$, minimum bulk and tail effective sample sizes above 400, minimum Bayesian fraction of missing information (BFMI) above $.64$, and no observation with Pareto $k>.7$.

The pointwise leave-one-out score vectors use the same trial ordering and response support.
Because observations are repeated within participants, paired score contributions are first summed within participant.
A participant-level Bayesian bootstrap then assigns Dirichlet weights to the 113 participant contributions over 200{,}000 draws.
Reported uncertainty comprises 95\% credible intervals and posterior probabilities for directional contrasts.
The predictive target is another response from a participant represented in the dataset, with that participant's remaining trials available to estimate their participant-specific parameters.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:appendix:robustness}

Five robustness checks address the participant-level structure of the architecture comparison, its distribution across adjective families, the fixed colour reliability, the condition-specific length and selection terms, and generalisation to new participants.
Unless stated otherwise, contrasts are context-fixed, summed within participant, and summarised with the participant-level Bayesian bootstrap described above.

\paragraph{Endpoint mixture benchmark.}
For each participant, the benchmark takes the higher held-out score of the global and the fully incremental model.
Because this choice is made after seeing the scores, the benchmark favours an account in which participants differ only in which endpoint they resemble.
The plan-guided model with participant-specific $\kappa_i$ predicted better than this benchmark, whereas the plan-guided model with a shared $\kappa$ did not (\Cref{tab:robustness-checks}).
The participant-specific model exceeded both endpoint models for 59 of the 113 participants.

\paragraph{Adjective families.}
The plan-guided advantage over the average of the two endpoint models was credible in each adjective family.
Within size--colour trials, it was larger for responses that contained a form adjective than for responses that did not, but it was credible in both.

\paragraph{Colour reliability.}
The plan-guided models with context-fixed and updating semantics were refitted with colour reliability $\nu_{\mathrm C}=.75$ instead of $.59$.
Both models predicted worse than at $\nu_{\mathrm C}=.59$ ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=-263.0$, 95\% CrI $[-365.7,-163.4]$ for context-fixed semantics), and updating still predicted worse than context-fixed semantics.
The shared $\kappa$ remained between the endpoints (posterior mean $.29$).

\paragraph{Condition-specific terms.}
The global, plan-guided, and fully incremental models were refitted under both semantic regimes with the two condition-specific length and selection terms fixed at zero.
These models predicted worse than their counterparts with the two terms, but plan-guided production still predicted better than both endpoints and context-fixed semantics still predicted better than updating.
The shared $\kappa$ remained between the endpoints (posterior mean $.50$, 95\% CrI $[.46,.55]$ under context-fixed semantics).

\paragraph{New participants.}
Participants were assigned to ten folds, stratified by size-discriminability group.
Each model was refitted without the participants of one fold, and every held-out participant was scored as a new speaker.
The score integrates over participant effects drawn from the fitted population distribution and is computed jointly over all of that participant's responses, using 400 posterior draws and 100 participant-effect draws per posterior draw.
All 50 fits had no divergences and a maximum $\widehat R$ of $1.011$; halving the number of participant-effect draws changed fold totals by less than $0.5$ ELPD.

\section{Local Normalisation and Model Endpoints}
\label{sec:appendix:normalisation-audit}

\paragraph{Local adjective competition.}
For the utilities defined in \Cref{eq:prefix-raw-utility}, taking the logarithm of the product of locally normalised choices gives the accumulated utility minus the local-normalisation term.
Suppressing the shared referent and scene arguments,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \log \prod_t
  \frac{\exp U_t(w_t\mid u_{<t})}
       {\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(u_{<t})}\exp U_t(a\mid u_{<t})}
  &= \sum_t U_t(w_t\mid u_{<t})
     - \sum_t\log\!\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(u_{<t})}
       \exp U_t(a\mid u_{<t}) \\
  &= U^{\mathrm{complete}}(u)
     - \mathcal N^{\mathrm{local}}(u).
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:local-normalisation}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Relation between the endpoints.}
At common parameter values, plan-guided production with $\kappa=0$ exactly recovers global production, and with $\kappa=1$ exactly recovers fully incremental production.
The stable-order score, length and selection terms, and utterance space are identical across the three architectures.

\paragraph{Plan-guided interpolation.}
Before the final Laplace smoothing, the same model can be written at common parameter values as a geometric interpolation between the global and fully incremental endpoints:
\begin{equation}
  S_{\kappa}(u\mid o^*,X)
  \propto
  S_{\mathrm{G}}(u\mid o^*,X)^{1-\kappa}
  S_{\mathrm{I}}(u\mid o^*,X)^{\kappa},
  \label{eq:plan-guided-interpolation}
\end{equation}
where $S_{\mathrm{G}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{I}}$ are the endpoint distributions evaluated with the same listener, stable-order component, length and selection terms, and parameter values.
The proportionality in \Cref{eq:plan-guided-interpolation} renormalises the interpolated scores over the fifteen completed utterances.


\section{Sequential Context Updating for Size}
\label{sec:appendix:semantic-details}

For the hierarchical semantic path, let $C_{j-1}$ be the context state after the syntactically inner modifiers have composed.
The semantic regime determines the comparison class used for size:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:hierarchical-size-regimes}
\begin{align}
  \llbracket \adj{S}\rrbracket_{\mathrm{fix}}(o)
  &=\llbracket \adj{S}\rrbracket_{\theta_{\delta}(\mathcal C)}(o),
  \label{eq:context-fixed}\\
  \llbracket \adj{S}\rrbracket_{\mathrm{upd},C_{j-1}}(o)
  &=\llbracket \adj{S}\rrbracket_{\theta_{\delta}(C_{j-1})}(o).
  \label{eq:context-updating}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\theta_{\delta}$ derives a graded size threshold from a comparison class and $\mathcal C$ is the original scene, represented by the uniform prior $P_0$.
Under updating, the literal interpretation recomputes the size threshold from the hierarchically prior state; under context-fixed semantics, it anchors the threshold to the scene prior.
At each surface position, the production calculation evaluates the current prefix using this noun-outward interpretation.

\section{Simulation Parameter Sweeps}
\label{sec:appendix:sim-params}

The original two-order simulation compares a global rule based on completed-utterance utility with an incremental rule that includes continuation-versus-stop competition.
We report its scene construction, definitions, and parameter sweeps first.
\Cref{sec:appendix:controlled-simulation} then introduces the production specification on the same scenes, as in the controlled comparison in \Cref{sec:simulation}.

Each random display contains between 2 and 30 objects whose sizes are drawn from a normal distribution centred at $15.5$ and truncated to $[1,30]$.
The largest object is designated as the target and assigned blue; each non-target object is independently assigned blue or non-blue with equal probability.
Three levels of contextual size spread vary how distinctly the largest target differs from its competitors.
The semantic grid varies colour reliability, size-threshold selectivity, and perceptual blur, which control the strength of colour-based restriction, how selectively \emph{big} applies, and how sharply size differences are represented.
In the simulation notation, $k$ corresponds to threshold selectivity $\delta$ and $w_f$ to perceptual blur $w$ in \Cref{eq:size-threshold,eq:size-membership}.

\Cref{fig:sim-k,fig:sim-wf,fig:sim-csv} show how the three fixed semantic parameters affect communicative success across the two speaker architectures.
Each figure separates the semantic regimes and averages equally over the other parameter settings.
For the two-order simulation, write $q(v,o)=L(o\mid v,X)^\alpha$, with $\alpha=1$ and equal utterance costs.
For distinct adjectives $a,b$, denote the original global and incremental rules by $S_G$ and $S_I$:
\begin{align}
 S_G(ab\mid o,X)&=\frac{q(ab,o)}{q(ab,o)+q(ba,o)},\\
 R_I(ab\mid o,X)&=\frac{q(a,o)}{q(a,o)+q(b,o)}
                  \frac{q(ab,o)}{q(ab,o)+q(a,o)},\\
 S_I(ab\mid o,X)&=\frac{R_I(ab\mid o,X)}{R_I(ab\mid o,X)+R_I(ba\mid o,X)}.
\end{align}
The second factor of $R_I$ is the continuation-versus-stop choice, and the final normalisation conditions on producing a completed two-adjective order.
The original global rule supplies the terminal utility used in the first row of Panel A in the main comparison.
The production global model accumulates utility across adjective positions; its incremental endpoint assigns utterance length through the completed-response distribution.
The pragmatic listener uses a uniform referent prior, giving $L^{\mathrm{prag}}(o\mid ab,X)=S(ab\mid o,X)/\sum_r S(ab\mid r,X)$.

The reproducible sweep uses seed 20260906 and samples 1{,}000 independent scenes for each context-size and size-spread combination.
Each scene is reused across semantic parameters, semantic regimes, and speakers, yielding 24{,}000 independent scenes and 12{,}000{,}000 model evaluations.
Monte Carlo standard errors are calculated after averaging parameter settings within scenes; repeated evaluations of one scene do not count as independent samples.

\subsection{Supplementary Predictions on Experimental Displays}
\label{sec:appendix:fitted-bridge}

The supplementary simulation uses the size--colour trials and all eight fitted production models.
For each model, 50 evenly spaced retained draws from each of its four chains give 200 posterior parameter draws.
The simulation preserves the fifteen named responses, fitted semantic constants, participant parameters, stable-order score, salience calculation, and length and selection terms.
For each trial and posterior draw, each of the six objects is evaluated as the intended referent while the adjective extensions and observed task covariates remain fixed.
Thus changing the candidate referent does not change which colour or form the uttered words denote.

Let $S_{it}^{(d)}(u\mid o,X)$ be the resulting complete-response probability at posterior draw $d$.
We evaluate the speaker's size-first probability conditional on the size--colour adjective set as
\begin{equation}
 \frac{S_{it}^{(d)}(\mathrm{SC}\mid o^*,X)}
 {S_{it}^{(d)}(\mathrm{SC}\mid o^*,X)+S_{it}^{(d)}(\mathrm{CS}\mid o^*,X)}.
\end{equation}
Separately, the pragmatic listener normalises the full-response speaker probabilities over the six referents using a uniform prior.
The listener therefore retains the fitted fifteen-response normalisation when evaluating each order.
We average each quantity over the original size--colour trials within posterior draw, then report the posterior mean and central 95\% interval.

\Cref{fig:fitted-simulation-bridge} reports the resulting speaker and listener predictions.
In the plan-guided models with participant-specific $\kappa_i$, the mean size-first preference in target probability was $.0062$ under fixed semantics, 95\% posterior interval $[.0047,.0077]$, and $.0058$ under updating, $[.0045,.0072]$.
The corresponding mean probabilities of size-first conditional on the size--colour adjective set were $.967$ and $.966$.
Both fitted regimes therefore generate a size-first preference under this counterfactual listener calculation.
In the response-score decomposition, the predictive difference between these models was concentrated in adjective content and length.
The two calculations describe how the models differ in predicting which utterance is produced while both assigning a listener preference for size-first order.
