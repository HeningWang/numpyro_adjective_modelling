\begin{abstract}
Adjective ordering preferences, such as placing size before colour, have been explained by two accounts of communicative efficiency.
Hierarchical composition favours placing less subjective adjectives closer to the noun, where they restrict the interpretation of more subjective ones; incremental pragmatics favours placing more discriminating adjectives earlier, where they help the listener narrow down the referent sooner.
However, the two accounts can favour opposing orders when the scene makes the less subjective adjective the more discriminating one.
Which order speakers produce depends on what they evaluate: the complete description as a whole, or each adjective incrementally?
The answer may lie in between.
We develop Rational Speech Act models of adjective production that formalise both accounts and span this range, from a global to an incremental speaker.
Between these endpoints, a plan-guided speaker chooses each adjective for its immediate usefulness while looking ahead to the complete description, so that both benefits can shape the order.
We found that the plan-guided speaker best predicted German production data, whereas modelling hierarchical composition added no predictive benefit.
Simulations nevertheless showed that composition can shape adjective order, but only under restricted conditions.
Together, these findings motivate an account of boundedly rational communication in which partial planning supports good-enough production: speakers anticipate the communicative value of a description while resolving individual word choices incrementally.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} adjective ordering, production planning, reference production, Rational Speech Act, Bayesian cognitive modelling
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\label{sec:background}
% =============================================================================

Speakers can begin an utterance before every part of it has been prepared and continue planning during articulation \citep{ferreira2002incremental,konopka2014priming}.
Adjective ordering shows the consequences of such incremental planning particularly clearly, although it has rarely been studied from this perspective.
In languages with prenominal modification, robust ordering preferences favour size-first sequences such as \emph{the big blue sticker} over \emph{the blue big sticker} \citep{sproat1991,scontras2023adjective}.
Yet the first adjective is also the listener's first descriptive cue to the target.
In a scene where colour alone identifies the intended sticker, \emph{blue} would provide the decisive information sooner, even though a colour-first sequence conflicts with the canonical size-first preference \citep{fukumura2018ordering,trotzke2019,rubio2021wordorder}.
The order that sounds most natural may therefore differ from the order that guides the listener most efficiently through the scene.

Do speakers decide on an order before they begin, or does the order emerge as they speak?
Because adjectives are produced incrementally, both are possible.
A speaker who has planned the complete description knows whether it will contain size, colour, or both, and can then decide how to order these properties.
A speaker who plans less may instead begin with the property that is most salient or most discriminating in the scene, so that the order follows from which adjectives are added next.
In the first case, order is a decision about a planned set of adjectives; in the second, it is a by-product of selection.
Which order a speaker produces may thus depend on how much of the description is planned in advance.

\label{sec:bg:subjectivity}
Two accounts of communicative efficiency address the tension between conventional and context-dependent efficient order, one based on hierarchical composition and one on incremental interpretation.
More subjective adjectives tend to occur farther from the noun, yielding a size-first preference for size and colour \citep{scontras2017subjectivity,trainin2021s}.
The first account connects this pattern to hierarchical semantic composition: the noun-adjacent adjective restricts the context in which an outer adjective is interpreted \citep{franke2019subjectivity,scontras2019grammatical,scontras2020incremental}.
In \emph{the big blue sticker}, \emph{blue} combines with \emph{sticker} before \emph{big}, potentially narrowing the comparison class against which size is evaluated.
Because colour is less subjective than size, evaluating \emph{big} among the blue objects can reduce uncertainty about its interpretation.

During incremental production, an adjective's usefulness also depends on when it becomes available in the surface sequence.
Listeners use partial linguistic input and visual context to restrict candidate referents before a noun phrase is complete \citep{tanenhaus1995integration,sedivy1999achieving}.
Placing the more discriminating property first can facilitate this process, favouring colour-first order when colour identifies the target sooner \citep{fukumura2018ordering,rubio2020incrementality,rubio2021wordorder}.
Hierarchical composition and surface production thus provide two directions in which adjective order can support efficient reference.
The two accounts thus presuppose the two ways in which an order can arise: the compositional benefit of size-first order depends on the complete phrase, whereas the incremental benefit of colour-first order depends on each adjective as it is produced.

\label{sec:bg:efficiency}
Both accounts compare two orders of the same adjectives, whereas in production the available orders depend on which properties are selected and how much information the description includes.
In a colour-sufficient scene, \emph{the blue sticker} may complete the description, so competition between size--colour and colour--size orders may never arise.
In a size-sufficient scene, speakers may still produce \emph{the big blue sticker}, including colour even when size alone identifies the target.
Colour is included redundantly more often than relative adjectives such as size \citep{tarenskeen2015overspecification,rubio2016redundant,degen2020redundancy,tourtouri2021rational}.
Such redundancy can itself arise in the two ways described above: a speaker may begin with salient colour before establishing that size is needed \citep{pechmann1989incremental}, or include colour because it helps the listener find the target \citep{rubio2016redundant,degen2020redundancy}.
Reference planning therefore involves selecting properties for mention as well as arranging them \citep{fukumura2010use,van2019conceptualization}.
Selection and ordering together make the full distribution of one-, two-, and three-adjective utterances the relevant production outcome.

\label{sec:bg:incremental}
The Rational Speech Act (RSA) framework can predict this distribution by relating speaker choice to an utterance's usefulness to a listener and its production cost \citep{frank2012predicting,goodman2016pragmatic,degen2023rational}.
\citet{schlotterbeck2023incremental} combined the two efficiency accounts in an incremental RSA model to capture size-first preferences and their modulation by referential context.
That account compared two fixed adjective orders, leaving unresolved how much speakers plan and how planning shapes the content and length of a description.
We extend this account by comparing fully planned (global) production, fully incremental production \citep{cohngordon2019incremental}, and an intermediate hypothesis that we call \emph{plan-guided incremental production}.
Under this hypothesis, a partial plan of the complete description guides each adjective, while immediate cues still compete.
The global and fully incremental endpoints correspond to the two ways in which an order can arise.
A free parameter determines the degree of planning between these endpoints.

Our central research question is: \emph{How much of a description do speakers plan when selecting and ordering adjectives?}
We combine two German slider-rating studies and a production study with Bayesian predictive model comparison and controlled simulation.
The slider studies measure order preferences when both complete descriptions are given; the production study shows which content, length, and order speakers produce when they must plan the description themselves.
We ask which degree of planning best predicts these descriptions and whether narrowing the comparison class for size, as proposed by the compositional account, improves prediction.
The simulation then examines how the communicative advantage of an order changes when shorter descriptions are also available.

\Cref{sec:task} introduces the task and response space, followed by the models and their degrees of planning in \Cref{sec:model}.
\Cref{sec:modelcomparison} presents the empirical evidence and predictive comparisons; \Cref{sec:simulation} examines the conditions for a communicative ordering advantage.
\Cref{sec:discussion} discusses the implications for production planning, reference production, and semantic composition.

\section{Referential Task}
\label{sec:task}

All three studies used the same referential task.
The task was presented as a reference game about collectible stickers: a partner saw the same stickers in a different order and lacked the target frame, making spatial descriptions uninformative.
On each critical trial, participants saw six objects with one framed target.
The noun was always \emph{Aufkleber} (`sticker'), and objects varied in size, colour, and form.
The target was always the largest object of its colour and form, so it could always be described as \emph{big}.

In Study~1a and its direct replication, Study~1b, participants used a continuous slider to indicate which of two phrases they preferred, such as \emph{den gro{\ss}en blauen Aufkleber} and \emph{den blauen gro{\ss}en Aufkleber} (`the big blue sticker' and `the blue big sticker').
In Study~2, participants saw the sentence frame \emph{den \_\_\_ Aufkleber} and typed adjectives into it to describe the target to their partner.
This response format allowed us to observe which properties they mentioned, how many adjectives they used, and how they ordered them.
Participant samples, behavioural results, and the fitting procedure are reported in \Cref{sec:modelcomparison}; full experimental details appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:methods-transparency}.

Each study used a 3 (adjective pair: size--colour, size--form, colour--form; within-subjects) $\times$ 3 (referential context: first property sufficient, second property sufficient, both necessary; within-subjects) $\times$ 2 (size discriminability: high, low; between-subjects) mixed design.
Referential context changed which properties were needed to identify the target; in size--colour trials, for example, size alone (size sufficient), colour alone (colour sufficient), or both properties (both necessary) identified the target.
Target--competitor size differences were more pronounced in the high-discriminability group than in the low-discriminability group (\Cref{fig:design}).
Referential context changes whether size is needed; discriminability changes how easily the size difference can be seen, while the adjective \emph{big} stays the same.
The behavioural analyses focus on size--colour trials, the classic contrast in the ordering literature and our motivating example.
The production models are fitted to all three adjective pairs, with colour--form trials providing a baseline without a subjective adjective.

A production response could contain a single adjective, such as \emph{blue}; two adjectives, such as \emph{big blue}; or three, such as \emph{big blue round}.
Coding each adjective as size (\adj{S}), colour (\adj{C}), or form (\adj{F}) gives fifteen ordered categories: three single adjectives, six two-adjective sequences, and six three-adjective sequences.
All production models assign probabilities to these fifteen categories.
They are fitted to Study~2; the slider studies provide separate evidence about explicit preferences between two competing orders.

\section{A Plan-Guided Production Model}
\label{sec:model}

Consider a scene in which colour alone identifies the target.
A fully planned speaker compares complete descriptions, such as \emph{blue}, \emph{big blue}, and \emph{blue big}, so a first adjective is likely when it begins good descriptions.
A fully incremental speaker evaluates the first adjective on its own, so \emph{blue} is likely because it immediately singles out the target, whatever follows.
Both speakers pursue the same goal of helping the listener identify the target; they differ only in how far ahead they look.
Which of them do human speakers resemble, or do they lie in between?

The Rational Speech Act (RSA) framework models a speaker who selects utterances by reasoning about how a literal listener would interpret them, favouring utterances that lead the listener to the intended referent at low cost \citep{frank2012predicting,goodman2016pragmatic}.
Standard RSA speakers evaluate complete utterances, whereas incremental variants evaluate one word at a time \citep{cohngordon2019incremental}.
Our model keeps this listener-oriented reasoning but treats the choice between these two forms of evaluation as a matter of degree, estimated from the data.
Because planning cannot be observed directly in typed descriptions, the model infers it from the distribution of descriptions that speakers produce: which properties they mention, how many adjectives they use, and how they order them.
The models also differ in whether the comparison class for size is updated as adjective meanings compose from the noun outwards.
Panel B of \Cref{fig:overview} shows these two dimensions.


\subsection{Literal Listener and Meaning Function}
\label{sec:model:response-space}
\label{sec:model:semantic-comparison}

All models use the observed six-object display.
Size has a graded meaning: an object counts as \emph{big} to the degree that it is larger than the other objects in its comparison class \citep{schmidt2009how,franke2019subjectivity}.
Colour meanings are graded as well: objects of the named colour receive most, but not all, of the weight.
Form is treated as uninformative in the literal semantics; speakers' use of form is captured by salience and the length and selection terms.
These semantic settings are shared across the production architectures.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
For every complete or partial adjective string, the literal listener $L$ interprets the adjectives by hierarchical composition, applying modification from the noun outwards.
For example, in \emph{big blue sticker}, \emph{blue} modifies \emph{sticker} before \emph{big} is interpreted.
Let $c_1,\ldots,c_T$ denote the modifiers of a string in this order, so that $c_1$ is the adjective next to the noun.
Starting from a uniform prior over the objects, $C_0=P_0$, composition updates a distribution over objects at each step:
\begin{equation}
  C_j(o)
  \propto
  C_{j-1}(o)\,
  \llbracket c_j\rrbracket_{\rho_j}(o),
  \qquad j=1,\ldots,T,
  \label{eq:hierarchical-composition}
\end{equation}
where $\llbracket c_j\rrbracket_{\rho_j}$ is the modifier's meaning under the selected semantic regime.
The final state gives the literal listener's distribution over objects.

Hierarchical composition affects order only if an inner adjective can change how an outer adjective is interpreted.
The two semantic regimes differ in exactly this respect.
Under \emph{context-fixed} semantics, the comparison class for size remains the whole scene.
Under \emph{sequential context updating}, the comparison class is recomputed after each modifier, so that in \emph{big blue}, \emph{big} is judged among objects weighted by how well they match \emph{blue}.
Because colour meanings are graded, this restriction is partial.
When it makes the target more distinctive in size, updating can make size-first order more helpful to the listener.
The threshold construction and semantic parameters appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:model-specification,sec:appendix:semantic-details}.

Interpretation proceeds from the noun outwards, but production proceeds from left to right.
The speaker evaluates the information available after each adjective in the surface sequence.
Let $u_{\leq t}=w_1\ldots w_t$ be the prefix comprising the first $t$ adjectives, and $P_0(o\mid X)=1/6$ the object prior in display $X$.
Each prefix is interpreted from this prior, applying each of its modifiers once in noun-outward order.
For \emph{big blue}, the first evaluation uses \emph{big} alone, and the second uses the composed meaning of \emph{big blue}.
Under context-fixed semantics, the listener after a prefix is
\begin{equation}
  L(o\mid u_{\leq t},X)
  \propto
  P_0(o\mid X)\prod_{j=1}^{t}
  \llbracket w_j\rrbracket_{\mathrm{fix}}(o).
  \label{eq:prefix-listener}
\end{equation}
Under updating, the comparison class for \emph{big} therefore changes as the prefix grows from \emph{big} to \emph{big blue}.
The probability that this listener assigns to the target is the communicative value of the prefix.

\subsection{Incremental Speaker and Plan-Guided Production}
\label{sec:model:architectures}

The three production architectures share a \emph{prefix evaluation} rule: the speaker evaluates how the adjectives in an utterance guide the listener to the target at each adjective position.
Accumulating these evaluations means that a description gains value when it identifies the target early, not only when it is complete.
The production architecture determines how this accumulated utility is compared with alternatives: across complete descriptions, or among the adjectives available at each point.
At position $t$, the utility of candidate adjective $a$ for target $o^*$ is
\begin{equation}
  U_t(a\mid u_{<t},o^*,X)
  = \alpha\log L(o^*\mid u_{<t}+a,X)
  + \lambda_{\mathrm{salience}}z_a,
  \label{eq:prefix-raw-utility}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ controls sensitivity to listener success and $z_a$ represents the visual salience of the candidate property.
The two terms capture what can make an adjective attractive as soon as it is produced: it discriminates the target, or it is visually salient.
For a completed utterance $u$, the model combines the accumulated utility of its selected adjectives with a term for competition among alternatives at each adjective position:
\begin{align}
  U^{\mathrm{complete}}(u,o^*,X)
  &=\sum_t U_t(w_t\mid u_{<t},o^*,X),\\
  \mathcal N^{\mathrm{local}}(u,o^*,X)
  &=\sum_t\log\!\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(u_{<t})}
    \exp U_t(a\mid u_{<t},o^*,X),
  \label{eq:path-components}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal A(u_{<t})$ contains the adjective types still available after the current prefix.
The complete-utterance utility $U^{\mathrm{complete}}$ is the sum of these prefix evaluations and is shared by all three production architectures.
The local-normalisation term $\mathcal N^{\mathrm{local}}$ records the utility of the competing adjectives at each position.
When adjectives compete at a given point, a selected adjective receives lower probability if other available adjectives are also useful there.
For example, \emph{big blue} and \emph{blue big} contain the same properties but face different competitors at the first position.
When colour alone identifies the target, \emph{blue} is a strong competitor, so \emph{big} is less likely to come first.
Two completed utterances with similar $U^{\mathrm{complete}}$ can therefore receive different probabilities because they pass through prefixes with different degrees of competition.
These two terms correspond to the two influences in plan-guided production: the value of the complete description and the competition among adjectives at each point.

The probability of a completed utterance is
\begin{equation}
  S_{\kappa}(u\mid o^*,X)
  \propto
  \exp\!\left\{
    U^{\mathrm{complete}}(u,o^*,X)
    -\kappa\mathcal N^{\mathrm{local}}(u,o^*,X)
    +\beta\Omega(u)
    +T(u,X)
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:plan-guided-speaker}
\end{equation}
The parameter $\kappa$ determines how strongly competition among the adjectives available at each point contributes to the probability of a completed utterance.
With $\kappa=0$, this competition drops out, and the \emph{global} speaker compares only the fifteen complete descriptions.
With $\kappa=1$, the \emph{fully incremental} speaker produces each adjective by comparing it with the adjectives available at that point, without looking ahead; the probability of a description then combines these local choices with the shared terms below.\footnote{This contrast corresponds to that between locally and globally normalised sequence models in natural language processing \citep{lafferty2001conditional,andor2016globally}.}
Intermediate values define the \emph{plan-guided} speaker, in which both comparisons contribute.
Lower values of $\kappa$ therefore correspond to a greater degree of planning.
Even the global speaker still evaluates each prefix; it differs only in comparing complete descriptions.

Besides informativeness and salience, the descriptions are shaped by tendencies that are not specific to planning: speakers follow conventional orders, avoid unnecessarily long descriptions, prefer a single adjective when it suffices, and add properties when size is hard to see.
Our account does not aim to derive these tendencies, but a model that ignored them would misattribute their effects to the degree of planning.
Following common practice in Bayesian cognitive modelling, we include them as auxiliary components that link the model to the task \citep{lee2013bayesian,franke2016task}: a fixed order score $\Omega(u)$ from a German language model, weighted by $\beta$, and length and selection terms $T(u,X)$.
Their parameters are estimated jointly with $\kappa$, so uncertainty about them is carried into the inference about planning.
Because these components are identical across architectures, the architectures differ only in how descriptions are evaluated.
Their specification and development history appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:model-specification,sec:appendix:auxiliary-models}.

\section{Empirical Evidence and Model Comparison}
\label{sec:experiments}
\label{sec:modelcomparison}
\label{sec:model:extended}

This section evaluates the production models against the German data.
The slider studies show how referential context shapes preferences between two complete orders; the production study additionally shows which properties speakers select and how long their descriptions are, and it therefore provides the data for model comparison.
After summarising the samples and observed patterns, we ask which degree of planning best predicts production, whether planning varies across speakers, and whether updating the comparison class for size improves prediction, before checking the predictions of the best-predicting model.
Full experimental methods, behavioural analyses, and secondary results appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:methods-transparency}.

\subsection{Studies and Observed Responses}

All studies were conducted online with native German speakers recruited via Prolific.
Study~1a used the dataset reported by \citet{schlotterbeck2023incremental}; Study~1b replicated the task and materials in an independent sample.
Study~2 elicited production, with participants from either slider study ineligible.
\Cref{tab:study-samples} distinguishes the size--colour subsets used for the behavioural analyses from the complete production dataset used to compare the RSA models.
The first author coded production responses into the fifteen categories in \Cref{sec:task}.
Full exclusion and coding criteria are reported in \Cref{sec:appendix:methods-transparency}.
In particular, the exclusion of production participants with more than $80\%$ identical responses selects a sample that varies its descriptions across trials.

\paragraph{Explicit ordering preferences.}
Both slider studies showed the same relation between referential context and adjective-order preference: size-first ratings were strongest when size alone identified the target and weakest when colour alone did (\Cref{fig:slider-replication}).
The joint posterior probability of the ordering size-sufficient $>$ both-necessary $>$ colour-sufficient exceeded $.999$ in Study~1a and was $.999$ in Study~1b.
A small size-first preference remained in colour-sufficient displays: the estimated centred rating was $0.054$ ($95\%$ credible interval [CrI] $[0.003,0.109]$) in Study~1a and $0.057$ ($95\%$ CrI $[0.001,0.116]$) in Study~1b, on a scale from $-0.5$ to $0.5$ with zero indicating neutrality.
The interaction between size discriminability and context observed in Study~1a was not clearly replicated in Study~1b.
The replicated result therefore concerns the context gradient and the residual size-first preference; the evidence for how discriminability modifies that gradient was mixed.

\paragraph{Adjective selection, length, and order.}
The production distribution in \Cref{fig:production-distribution} shows how referential context shaped what speakers chose to mention.
Both-necessary scenes generally elicited longer descriptions, whereas one-property-sufficient scenes more often elicited a single adjective.
Colour-sufficient scenes were commonly resolved with colour alone, leaving no size--colour ordering decision to observe on those trials.
Conditional on producing both size and colour, size-first descriptions remained more frequent and colour-first descriptions were comparatively rare.

Perceptual reliability also affected the descriptions.
Within size-sufficient displays, lower discriminability increased the estimated probability of redundant adjective use by $23.4$ percentage points ($95\%$ CrI $[15.1,31.8]$).
Within both-necessary displays, higher discriminability increased the probability of a size-initial response by $14.1$ percentage points ($95\%$ CrI $[3.6,24.2]$).
Both effects varied credibly across contexts in the planned interaction contrasts (\Cref{tab:production-behavioural-contrasts}).
Redundancy means mentioning more properties than required for unique reference; size-initiality includes responses consisting of size alone and therefore reflects selection as well as order.
The production models must account for this joint distribution of content, length, and order, including the redundant descriptions whose frequency varies across participants.

\subsection{Inference and Predictive Comparison}
\label{sec:model:development}
\label{sec:model:inference}

We fitted the production models to the same 9{,}100 annotated responses from 113 participants in Study~2, including size--colour, size--form, and colour--form trials.
Each response was represented as one of the fifteen ordered adjective categories used by every model.
Bayesian inference was performed in NumPyro \citep{phan2019composable}.
The slider ratings were analysed separately with behavioural hierarchical models, as specified in \Cref{sec:appendix:methods-transparency}.
The six matched production models and two participant-specific refinements follow the restrictions introduced in \Cref{sec:model}.

Approximate leave-one-out expected log predictive density (ELPD) measures how much probability each model assigns to held-out responses \citep{vehtari2017practical}.
Higher ELPD indicates better prediction of the complete utterance distribution.
The predictive target is another response from a participant represented in the dataset, with that participant's remaining trials available to estimate their participant-specific parameters.
Model development used these data, and response-wise leave-one-out prediction evaluates the specifications selected through that development.
Uncertainty in paired model comparisons is estimated with a participant-level Bayesian bootstrap and reported as 95\% credible intervals.
The same predictive target and uncertainty calculation are used throughout the architecture and hierarchy comparisons.
The inference procedure and numerical diagnostics appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:model-diagnostics}.

\subsection{Production Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture-comparison}

The Bayesian model comparison favoured plan-guided production over both global and fully incremental production (\Cref{fig:production-architecture-results}, Panel A).
Averaged across semantic regimes, the plan-guided model had higher predictive accuracy than the global model ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=256.9$, 95\% CrI $[71.8,404.7]$) and the fully incremental model ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=548.5$, 95\% CrI $[349.8,795.1]$).
These contrasts supported the plan-guided production hypothesis that complete-utterance evaluation and competition during incremental adjective choice jointly shape the response distribution.
In the context-fixed model, the posterior mean shared incremental-choice weight was $\kappa=.446$, with a 95\% CrI of $[.407,.486]$ (Panel B).
The estimate lay between the global and fully incremental endpoints, indicating an intermediate contribution of local competition within the fitted architecture.

\label{sec:architecture-residuals}
The predictive differences were most apparent in form-involving trials, where colour and form competed for the initial position.
Colour-initial responses accounted for $81.7\%$ of the plan-guided model's ELPD advantage over the average of the global and fully incremental models.
Different opening adjectives leave different continuations available, allowing competition at later positions to affect the probabilities of the completed descriptions.
The advantage therefore extended beyond the motivating size--colour contrast to the selection and ordering of properties in the broader response space.

\subsection{Plan-Guided Production and Participant Variation}
\label{sec:participant-variation}

Allowing incremental-choice weights to vary across participants yielded a credible improvement over the otherwise matched model with a shared weight.
Under context-fixed semantics, the participant-specific model gained $364.7$ ELPD (95\% CrI $[248.2,521.0]$); the gain under sequential context updating was similar.
The posterior mean population location remained intermediate between the global and fully incremental endpoints ($\mu_{\kappa}=.472$, 95\% CrI $[.337,.618]$), alongside substantial variation in participant-specific weights.
Thus, the improvement came from accommodating differences across participants while retaining an intermediate population location.

The predictive benefit was concentrated in two- and three-adjective utterances, whereas predictions of single-adjective responses became less accurate.
The gains for longer utterances outweighed this loss and extended to three-adjective descriptions, which contain redundant information in the present task.
Participant variation therefore improved the model's account of descriptions involving several adjective choices, including the selection and ordering of additional properties.
\Cref{sec:appendix:participant-audit} reports the response-length contrasts and participant-level diagnostics.
\label{sec:production-scope}

\subsection{The Contribution of Semantic Updating}
We next asked whether updating the size comparison class improves prediction of production.
The comparison favoured context-fixed semantics over sequential context updating under all three production architectures (\Cref{fig:production-architecture-results}, Panel C).
The average updating-minus-fixed contrast was negative ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=-43.5$, 95\% CrI $[-56.0,-31.8]$), indicating a credible predictive advantage for the context-fixed models.
This advantage remained in the participant-specific plan-guided comparison.

Decomposing the participant-specific model comparison showed that the semantic difference chiefly concerned how much speakers said and which properties they mentioned.
Context-fixed semantics improved predictions of utterance length ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=21.5$, 95\% CrI $[12.4,30.9]$) and adjective content conditional on length ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=31.1$, 95\% CrI $[23.5,39.2]$).
For order conditional on adjective content, the estimated advantage was small and its interval included zero ($\Delta\mathrm{ELPD}=1.5$, 95\% CrI $[-1.8,4.9]$).
The comparison therefore did not establish a clear predictive advantage for either semantic regime in conditional order.

Both models also showed response-specific discrepancies, including overprediction of three-adjective descriptions in size-sufficient size--form trials.
Full contrasts, the score decomposition, and the residual analysis appear in \Cref{sec:semantic-score-decomposition}.

\subsection{Predictive Adequacy of the Best-Predicting Model}

Posterior predictive checks assessed how closely the model reproduced the observed context sensitivity of adjective selection, utterance length, and initial position.
The plan-guided model with context-fixed semantics and participant-specific incremental-choice weights captured the broad context-dependent shifts in size-initial responses and redundant adjective use (\Cref{fig:production-architecture-ppc}, Panel A).
The comparison across all fifteen utterances and eighteen experimental conditions showed correspondence between observed and predicted proportions, alongside discrepancies for particular responses (Panel B).

\section{Simulation: Conditions for a Size-First Advantage}
\label{sec:simulation}

Earlier simulations found an average referential advantage for orders that place more subjective adjectives farther from the noun, motivating a pressure that could gradually stabilise ordering preferences across repeated language use \citep{franke2019subjectivity}.
Sequential context updating provides a mechanism for this advantage: the noun-adjacent adjective restricts the context in which an outer, more subjective adjective is interpreted.
The production findings place this proposal within a broader set of choices, because speakers also decide which adjectives to include and how much to say.
We therefore ask how the ordering advantage changes when the speaker evaluates information at each adjective position and can choose descriptions of different lengths.

\subsection{Controlled Changes in Evaluation and Available Descriptions}
\label{sec:simulation:boundary}

The simulation reuses the same 24{,}000 randomly generated displays across all comparisons.
Displays vary in object count and size distribution; the largest object is the target and is assigned blue.
Varying the number of blue distractors changes how strongly colour restricts the comparison class for size.
The scene-generation procedure and semantic parameter grid appear in \Cref{sec:appendix:sim-params}.

We introduce the production model's assumptions in controlled steps (\Cref{fig:simulation-controlled}, Panel A).
The first comparison gives a global speaker two alternatives, \emph{big blue} and \emph{blue big}, evaluated through the literal listener's target probability after the completed utterance.
The second retains global choice ($\kappa=0$) but introduces the prefix evaluation in \Cref{sec:model:architectures}, summing listener log probabilities at each adjective position.
For \emph{big blue}, the utility then includes the information supplied by \emph{big} as well as by the completed description.
Holding global choice fixed isolates the contribution of this prefix information.
We next add the single-adjective alternatives \emph{big} and \emph{blue}, and finally expand to the fifteen utterances used in production.
These four comparisons average over the same grid of 125 semantic parameter settings under each semantic regime.

A further comparison holds the fifteen utterances and production semantic constants fixed while varying the contribution of local competition: global ($\kappa=0$), plan-guided ($\kappa=.5$), and fully incremental ($\kappa=1$) production (Panel B).
All three architectures use prefix evaluation.
Throughout these comparisons, pragmatic optimality is $\alpha=1$, order, salience, length, and selection terms are zero, and final Laplace smoothing is omitted.
\Cref{sec:appendix:controlled-simulation} gives the full specification.

The pragmatic listener $L^{\mathrm{prag}}$ applies Bayes' rule to the speaker distribution, using an equal prior over objects.
An object receives more probability when a speaker intending that object would be more likely to produce the observed utterance.
For each context, the size-first advantage compares the listener's target probability after the two orders:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{SF}}
  =L^{\mathrm{prag}}(o^*\mid\text{``big blue''})
  -L^{\mathrm{prag}}(o^*\mid\text{``blue big''}).
  \label{eq:simulation-advantage}
\end{equation}
Positive values favour size-first order.
Other available descriptions can affect this measure because the listener reasons about the speaker's choice among them.

\subsection{Conditions under which an Order Asymmetry Emerges}
\label{sec:simulation:conditions}

With global choice and complete-utterance evaluation, sequential context updating generated a mean size-first advantage of approximately $1.1$ percentage points, while context-fixed semantics yielded no difference between the orders (\Cref{fig:simulation-controlled}, Panel A).
With fixed meanings, the two adjective orders have the same completed interpretation and therefore the same target probability under this evaluation rule.
Updating breaks this symmetry because colour restricts the comparison class for size.
This comparison recovers the proposed communicative contribution of hierarchical restriction.

Including information available at each adjective position changed the result.
With only the two orders available, the global production model yielded a mean colour-first advantage under both semantic regimes: approximately $2.7$ percentage points with fixed meanings and $1.8$ with updating.
The first adjectives can differ in informativeness even when the completed meanings coincide, allowing an ordering asymmetry under fixed semantics.
Its direction, however, depends on the alternatives available for reference.
Adding the single-adjective responses changed the mean advantage to size-first under both regimes, to approximately $2.3$ and $2.8$ percentage points, respectively.
The speaker can now identify a referent with one adjective, changing how probability is distributed over the two-adjective descriptions and how the pragmatic listener interprets their use.
Adjective selection and utterance length thus affect the communicative value of an order even when the two orders themselves remain unchanged.

Expanding to fifteen utterances retained the mean size-first advantage, at approximately $2.6$ percentage points with fixed semantics and $3.1$ with updating.
At the semantic constants used in production, all three production architectures generated a mean size-first advantage under both semantic regimes (\Cref{fig:simulation-controlled}, Panel B).
The advantage was approximately $1.9$ percentage points for global production, $1.5$ for plan-guided production, and $1.0$ for fully incremental production, with nearly identical values for fixed and updating semantics within each architecture.
Increasing the contribution of local adjective competition reduced the magnitude of the advantage while preserving its direction in this comparison.
Updating's additional contribution therefore depends on the semantic settings, and the size-first advantage in the broader utterance space extends across the three production architectures.

The simulation shows how the interpretation and selection of adjectives jointly determine the communicative advantage of an order.
Semantic updating can generate an advantage when comparing completed meanings; evaluating information available at each adjective position and allowing shorter descriptions changes both the source and direction of that advantage.
The effect of adding single-adjective alternatives gives a computational basis for considering order within the broader production distribution: the option of using a shorter description affects how the listener interprets the same two-adjective utterances.

\section{General Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The present findings connect adjective ordering to the cognitive organisation of reference production.
Stable size-first preferences coexist with context-sensitive choices of descriptive content and length.
Among the matched production models, the best predictions combine evaluation of complete descriptions with continued influence from local adjective competition.
Participant-specific weights further improve prediction, particularly for descriptions containing several adjectives.
These findings support a plan-guided account of production in which speakers anticipate the communicative value of a description while resolving individual choices incrementally.

\subsection{Bounded Planning and Redundant Descriptions}

A useful description must be selected from alternatives that differ in content, length, and order.
As the number of available properties increases, their combinations and orderings grow combinatorially, making exhaustive comparison increasingly demanding.
The cognitive problem is how speakers preserve sensitivity to the communicative consequences of a description while limiting the alternatives and continuations they evaluate.
Computational tractability constrains the processes and representations that can support this behaviour \citep{vanrooij2008tractable}; approximation alone provides no general guarantee of tractability \citep{kwisthout2011bayesian}.

Plan-guided production motivates a hypothesis about how planning could be organised under these constraints.
An utterance-level plan could specify properties relevant to identifying the target, constraining the alternatives considered during incremental word choice.
Local choices could remain sensitive to the developing description and the properties still available for mention.
This organisation accords with evidence that conceptual planning and linguistic formulation can proceed with different scopes \citep{griffin2000eyes,ferreira2002incremental,konopka2014priming}.
It also connects to good-enough processing, in which representations can remain partial while serving the current task \citep{ferreira2002goodenough}.

Redundant descriptions provide a case in which planning and communicative goals interact.
A property may be selected because it becomes accessible early, because it helps the listener identify the target, or because both pressures favour its inclusion \citep{pechmann1989incremental,engelhardt2006speakers,rubio2016redundant,degen2020redundancy}.
Readily available information can guide visual search or hedge uncertainty about another property \citep{rubio2019overinformative,tourtouri2021rational}.
The improvement from participant-specific incremental-choice weights suggests that the balance represented by the model varies across speakers.
Its gains for longer descriptions, alongside poorer prediction of single-adjective responses, locate this improvement in choices involving several adjectives.

The present model specifies a distribution over completed utterances and evaluates the full response space.
A process account must additionally explain how speakers generate candidate descriptions and allocate computation to evaluating them.
A resource-rational approach would relate the benefit of further planning to its time and processing costs \citep{lieder2020resource}.
Manipulating planning time and the number of relevant properties could test whether information available in later adjectives changes earlier choices.
Speech onset and eye movements could establish whether a redundant property was available before production began or entered consideration during the utterance.
Such evidence would connect the fitted balance between complete-description utility and local competition to the time course and resources of planning.

\subsection{Semantic Stability during Incremental Production}

The context-fixed models predicted production more accurately than models that recomputed the size comparison class during composition.
For the participant-specific models, this advantage chiefly concerned adjective content and utterance length; the difference in order conditional on content was small and uncertain.
The result makes the maintenance of a scene-based size comparison a plausible component of reference production in this task.
A speaker could establish which objects count as large or small during scene apprehension and retain that comparison while selecting adjectives.

The full scene and marked target provide information from which this comparison can be formed before production begins.
In discourse, a subset of referents may become relevant through a preceding utterance, potentially changing the context against which size is evaluated.
Varying when contextual information becomes available would help identify when speakers retain an initial comparison and when they revise it.
Independently varying lexical subjectivity and visual discriminability would further distinguish uncertainty about a property's interpretation from difficulty perceiving it in a particular scene.
The current visual manipulation holds lexical subjectivity constant, so its results bear on perceptual reliability within these materials.

\subsection{Available Alternatives and Communicative Efficiency}

The simulation shows that the availability of shorter descriptions changes the communicative advantage of the same two adjective orders.
Under complete-utterance evaluation, updating can generate a size-first advantage by changing the comparison class for size.
With prefix evaluation, adding single-adjective alternatives changes the average advantage from colour-first to size-first under both semantic regimes.
The value of an order therefore depends on which other descriptions the speaker can choose and which alternatives the listener expects the speaker to have considered.
This dependence gives a computational basis for studying ordering within the broader distribution of content and length.

An account of reference production must consequently explain how alternatives become available.
Visual attention, lexical accessibility, and discourse can influence which properties enter consideration and how they are combined into candidate descriptions.
An utterance-level plan could direct consideration towards properties relevant to the referential goal, while local competition influences which property is mentioned next.
Making shorter alternatives more or less available, while holding the scene and two-adjective sequences constant, would test their contribution to interpretation.
Comparing production and comprehension under this manipulation would examine whether speakers and listeners rely on corresponding alternative sets.

The predictive findings are conditional on the shared model specification and the response format examined here.
The models include stable order expectations, salience, and length and selection terms selected during development with these data; form contributes through salience and the length and selection terms under a neutral literal likelihood.
Response-wise leave-one-out prediction evaluates further responses from participants represented in the dataset.
Freer production with new participants, materials, and languages would test how the plan-guided account extends beyond these German prenominal descriptions.
Together with the remaining predictive discrepancies, these scope conditions define where further evidence can refine the account.

The broader contribution is an account of adjective ordering embedded in the construction of a useful referential description.
Scene-based meanings, stable order expectations, and incremental competition jointly shape what speakers choose to mention and how they arrange it.
The plan-guided framework connects these choices to the evaluation of a complete description, while the simulation shows how available alternatives affect its interpretation.
This combination links adjective ordering to how much speakers plan and how individual choices unfold during communication.

\subsection{Design and Hypotheses}

The studies shared the reference-game setting and display design described in \Cref{sec:task}.
For slider judgements, greater referential usefulness of size predicts stronger size-first preference, yielding the ordering size-sufficient $>$ both-necessary $>$ colour-sufficient; a stable size-first preference predicts ratings above neutrality even when colour alone identifies the target.
The slider interaction contrasts assess how discriminability effects differ across contexts, with Study~1b testing whether the pattern observed in Study~1a recurs.
For production, the targeted interaction contrasts evaluate whether lower size discriminability increases redundancy more strongly when size alone is sufficient, and whether higher discriminability increases size-initiality more strongly when both properties are necessary.
These outcomes connect the selection and ordering of properties to the referential demands of the display.

\subsection{Procedure}

Each study began with three practice trials with feedback.
Main trials were divided into four blocks of approximately equal length, with self-paced breaks and independently randomised trial order within each block.
Study~1a participants completed 81 critical trials and 99 filler or control trials (180 total), with a mean completion time of 27.6 minutes.
Control trials had an unambiguous expected response; the additional ``none fits'' option allowed participants to indicate that neither description identified the target.
Frequent use of this option on experimental trials was an exclusion criterion, as detailed in \Cref{tab:exclusion-transparency}.
Study~1b used the same 1{,}080 combinations of list, item, and condition as Study~1a.

On each critical trial, two phrases differing in adjective order appeared on opposite sides of a continuous slider, together with a ``none of the descriptions fits'' option.
Left--right presentation was randomised across trials.
Ratings were centred so that zero indicated neutrality, positive values favoured size-first, and negative values favoured colour-first order, on a scale from $-0.5$ to $0.5$.

Participants typed adjective material into the frame \emph{den \_\_\_ Aufkleber}.
Practice trials introduced the relevant colour and form vocabulary.
Study~2 participants completed 81 critical and 54 filler or control trials (135 total), with a mean completion time of 32.4 minutes.
Fewer filler and control trials were used because typed responses were slower and more effortful than slider judgements.
Compensation followed the same rate as Study~1a.
Restricting responses to the adjective slot reduced off-task variability and provided a controlled basis for annotation.
Annotation rules and the retained response categories are given below.

\subsection{Data Analysis}

\paragraph{Response coding.}
The first author annotated production responses using the 15 ordered adjective categories listed in \Cref{tab:production-coding}.
The data files use \texttt{D} for the size or dimension adjective.

\paragraph{Behavioural models.}
The behavioural hypotheses were evaluated with Bayesian hierarchical models.
All behavioural analyses used the size--colour subsets: 3{,}485 observations in Study~1a, 2{,}786 in Study~1b, and 3{,}034 in Study~2.
The production-model comparison addresses predictive differences among the proposed computational mechanisms over the complete fifteen-category response space and all 9{,}100 production trials.
For the slider analyses, ratings were reoriented toward size-first, divided by 100, and centred by subtracting $0.5$.
Let $\mathbf{x}_{ij}$ encode referential context, size discriminability, and their interaction, and let $\mathbf{z}_{ij}$ encode the participant intercept and context slopes.
The slider models used a Gaussian likelihood with
\[
  y_{ij}\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_{ij},\sigma),
  \qquad
  \mu_{ij}=\mathbf{x}_{ij}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta+\mathbf{z}_{ij}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}_{i},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{b}_{i}\sim\operatorname{MVN}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol\Sigma_{\mathrm{participant}}).
\]
For production, size-initiality indicated whether the coded response began with the size adjective.
Redundant adjective use, coded as over-informativeness in the analysis, indicated a three-adjective response in both-necessary contexts or a multi-adjective response in one-property-sufficient contexts.
Both binary outcomes used a Bernoulli likelihood with a logit link and an item intercept $c_k$:
\[
  y_{ijk}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\pi_{ijk}),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{logit}(\pi_{ijk})=\mathbf{x}_{ijk}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta+\mathbf{z}_{ijk}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}_{i}+c_k,
  \qquad
  c_k\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{item}}).
\]
For the slider studies, the interaction was represented by two posterior contrasts: the high-minus-low discriminability effect in the both-necessary context minus the corresponding effect in the size-sufficient context, and the high-minus-low effect in the colour-sufficient context minus the effect in the size-sufficient context.
The production interactions were planned contrasts on the posterior-predictive probability scale.
For size-initiality, the contrast was the high-minus-low discriminability effect in the both-necessary context minus the mean effect in the two one-property-sufficient contexts.
For redundant adjective use, the contrast was the low-minus-high discriminability effect in the size-sufficient context minus the mean effect in the both-necessary and colour-sufficient contexts.

The slider models used a Gaussian likelihood, correlated participant intercepts and context slopes, fixed-effect priors $\operatorname{Normal}(0,.30)$, $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.20)$ priors on participant standard deviations, and a $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.25)$ prior on residual standard deviation.
The production models used Bernoulli likelihoods, the same correlated participant effects, an item intercept, a $\operatorname{Normal}(0,2)$ intercept prior, $\operatorname{Normal}(0,1.5)$ priors on the remaining fixed effects, and $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(1)$ priors on participant and item standard deviations.
Participant-effect correlations received an LKJ$(2)$ prior.
Four chains run in parallel each provided $1{,}000$ retained draws after $1{,}000$ warm-up iterations.
All four fits had zero divergences, no maximum-tree-depth hits, $\widehat{R}\leq1.01$, and minimum effective sample sizes above $900$.
For each posterior draw, posterior-predictive cell expectations were computed for a new participant and, in production, a new item by integrating over the fitted random-effects distributions.
The planned contrasts were then evaluated on the rating or response-probability scale.

\section{Production Model Specification}
\label{sec:appendix:model-specification}
\label{sec:appendix:inventory}

\subsection{Representation of Experimental Displays}

Participant list, condition, and item identifiers link each retained response to its recorded six-object display.
The model represents each display as a $6\times3$ array of size, colour, and form values, with the intended referent indexed first.
This representation preserves the observed values of all three properties, including those outside the adjective pair defining the trial.

\subsection{Graded Size Meaning}

Size is represented by a graded threshold over the comparison class $\mathcal C$ \citep{schmidt2009how,franke2019subjectivity}:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{\delta}(\mathcal C)
  = Q_{.80}(\mathcal C)
    - \delta\,\bigl(Q_{.80}(\mathcal C)-Q_{.20}(\mathcal C)\bigr),
  \label{eq:size-threshold}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{.20}$ and $Q_{.80}$ are probability-weighted size quantiles and $\delta$ controls threshold selectivity.
For an object of size $s_o$, graded membership is
\begin{equation}
 m_{\mathrm S}(o\mid\mathcal C)
 =\Phi\!\left(\frac{s_o-\theta_\delta(\mathcal C)}
 {w\sqrt{s_o^2+\theta_\delta(\mathcal C)^2+\epsilon}}\right),
 \qquad w=.6856,\quad\epsilon=10^{-8},
 \label{eq:size-membership}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.
The weighted quantiles are the first observed sizes at which cumulative comparison-class probability reaches $.20$ and $.80$.
The same width $w$ is used in both discriminability groups; the recorded sizes enter this function directly.
For colour and form, matching objects receive membership $\nu$ and nonmatching objects $1-\nu$, with $\nu_{\mathrm C}=.59$ and $\nu_{\mathrm F}=.50$.
Each prefix is interpreted from the uniform object prior, applying its modifiers once in noun-outward order.
Context updating changes the comparison-class weights used for the size quantiles as inner modifiers are applied.
\Cref{eq:prefix-listener,eq:prefix-raw-utility,eq:path-components,eq:plan-guided-speaker} define how this meaning enters the literal listener $L$ and the three production accounts.

\Cref{fig:production-inference-plate} separates fixed theoretical choices, estimated parameters, participant variation, and observed responses.
The fixed variables $A$ and $R$ index production architecture and semantic regime.
For participant $i$ on trial $t$, the model maps the display and target $X_{it}$ and the stable-order score $\Omega(u)$ to a probability $\pi_{it}(u)$ over the fifteen possible utterances, from which the observed response $y_{it}$ is drawn.
The shared coefficients govern visual salience, adjective selection, and utterance length.

The population pragmatic-optimality parameter has prior $\alpha_0\sim\operatorname{HalfNormal}(3)$, and the population log stable-order weight has prior $\mu_{\log\beta}\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,.35)$.
Participant effects are noncentred: $\alpha_i=\alpha_0\exp(\tau_{\log\alpha}z_{\alpha i})$ and $\beta_i=\exp(\mu_{\log\beta}+\tau_\beta z_{\beta i})$, with independent standard-normal $z$ values, $\tau_{\log\alpha}\sim\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.15)$, and $\tau_\beta\sim\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.5)$.
The shared plan-guided weight has prior $\kappa\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$.
In the model with participant-specific incremental-choice weights, $\mu_\kappa\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $\kappa_i=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\operatorname{logit}(\mu_\kappa)+\tau_\kappa z_{\kappa i})$, with $\tau_\kappa\sim\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.5)$ and independent $z_{\kappa i}\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)$.
The salience coefficient has a $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(1)$ prior and the salience-continuation coefficient a $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(.75)$ prior.
The sufficient-single-adjective and form-use coefficients each have a $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(1.5)$ prior; the three-adjective penalty and the two size-reliability coefficients each have a $\operatorname{HalfNormal}(1)$ prior.
Threshold selectivity is fixed at $\delta=.50$; colour reliability, form reliability, and perceptual blur take the values specified above.
A final Laplace smoothing step mixes the predicted distribution with a uniform distribution over the fifteen responses, with a fixed weight of $.003$ on the uniform component.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Visual salience.}
The salience feature combines a fixed preference across properties with their contrast in the displayed scene.
For the target $o^*$, let $s_o$, $c_o$, and $f_o$ be an object's recorded size, colour, and form, and let $h$ indicate high size discriminability.
The three contrast scores are
\begin{align}
 r_{\mathrm S}&=\frac{1+h}{2}\,
 \operatorname{logistic}\!\left(
 \frac{s_{o^*}-\max_{o\ne o^*}s_o}{\operatorname{sd}(s)+10^{-8}}
 \right),\\
 r_{\mathrm C}&=1-\frac{1}{5}\sum_{o\ne o^*}\mathbf 1\{c_o=c_{o^*}\},
 &r_{\mathrm F}&=1-\frac{1}{5}\sum_{o\ne o^*}\mathbf 1\{f_o=f_{o^*}\}.
\end{align}
Here $\operatorname{sd}(s)$ is the population standard deviation across the six displayed sizes.
The fixed baseline scores are $(b_{\mathrm S},b_{\mathrm C},b_{\mathrm F})=(0,1,.25)$.
Centring across the three properties gives
\[
 z_a=b_a+r_a-\frac13\sum_v(b_v+r_v).
\]
A property receives greater salience when it contrasts with the distractors; the size contrast is additionally scaled by discriminability.
The same feature enters prefix utility through $\lambda_{\mathrm{salience}}z_a$ and the continuation cost in the length and selection terms below.

\paragraph{Stable order expectations.}
We derived the fixed order feature from \texttt{dbmdz/german-gpt2}.
For each pairing of six colour and six form adjectives, the scoring script generated all fifteen non-empty orders using those adjectives and \emph{großer}, followed by the noun \emph{Aufkleber}, without a surrounding sentence.
It evaluated the resulting 540 strings, including repeated shorter strings, using mean token surprisal in bits.
We averaged this surprisal within each order category and normalised $2^{-\overline{s}(u)}$ across the fifteen categories to obtain the fixed weights $q(u)$.
For $\mathcal U_{A(u)}$, the set of orders expressing the same adjective set as $u$, the model uses
\begin{equation}
 \Omega(u)=\log q(u)-\frac{1}{|\mathcal U_{A(u)}|}
 \sum_{v\in\mathcal U_{A(u)}}\log q(v).
 \label{eq:order-residual}
\end{equation}
This centring preserves relative order preferences within an adjective set and gives every single-adjective response a score of zero.
The scoring script, archived token-surprisal table, aggregation code, and fixed scores and residuals are supplied with the model code.
Participant-specific $\beta_i$ determines the influence of these stable expectations when the model normalises across the complete response space.

\paragraph{Length and selection terms.}
The length and selection terms $T(u,X)$ connect the referential design to choices about which properties to mention and how far to continue a description.
Let $n=|u|$ and $A(u)$ denote the length and unordered adjective set of $u$.
For this term, $X$ includes the display's condition labels: $d\in\{\adj{S},\adj{C},\adj{F},\varnothing\}$ is the property that identifies the target on its own, with $d=\varnothing$ in both-necessary conditions; $c$ indicates a colour-sufficient condition; and $h=1$ indicates high size discriminability ($h=0$ for low).
Using $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ for an indicator, the production models use
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T(u,X)={}&-\rho_{\mathrm{cont}}\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\max(z_{w_t},0)
 +\lambda_{\mathrm{suff}}\mathbf{1}\{d\ne\varnothing,\ u=(d)\}\\
&+\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}(1-c)\mathbf{1}\{\adj{F}\in A(u)\}
 -\lambda_{3}\mathbf{1}\{n=3\}\\
&+\lambda_{\mathrm{S}}h\,\mathbf{1}\{d=\adj{S},\ u=(\adj{S})\}\\
&+\lambda_{\mathrm{SF}}(1-2h)\mathbf{1}\{d=\adj{S},\ A(u)=\{\adj{S},\adj{F}\}\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:task-link}
\end{equation}
The continuation cost sums the positive salience scores of adjectives followed by another adjective, using the same $z$ scores as the prefix utility in \Cref{eq:prefix-raw-utility}.
The sufficiency term favours a single adjective that identifies the target, while the form term increases the value of form-containing descriptions outside colour-sufficient conditions.
With form reliability fixed at $.50$, form gives equal literal support to matching and nonmatching objects; this task term provides a direct contribution to form use.
The three-adjective penalty offsets the value accumulated along longer descriptions.
In size-sufficient conditions, the final two terms additionally favour the single size adjective at high discriminability and favour size--form pairs at low discriminability while penalising them at high discriminability.
The pair term applies equally to both orders.
All six coefficients are nonnegative and shared across participants within each fitted model, with the priors given above.
These terms enter the completed-utterance score alongside accumulated prefix utility, local competition, and the stable-order score; normalisation over all fifteen responses turns their combined values into predictions of adjective selection, length, and order.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Numerical evaluation and response likelihood.}
The implementation uses 64-bit arithmetic and a floor of $10^{-8}$ for size memberships, listener target probabilities before taking logarithms, and local choice probabilities.
Colour and form memberships receive an additive $10^{-8}$ offset.
Writing $g(u)$ for accumulated prefix utility, $i(u)$ for the sum of floored local log-choice probabilities, and $B(u)=\beta_i\Omega(u)+T(u,X)$, the computed score before Laplace smoothing is $(1-\kappa_i)g(u)+\kappa_i i(u)+B(u)$.
Normalising these scores yields $\widetilde S_{\kappa_i}(u\mid X)$, whose endpoints obey the geometric interpolation in \Cref{eq:plan-guided-interpolation}.
The categorical response likelihood is
\begin{equation}
 p(y_{it}=u\mid X_{it})=(1-.003)\widetilde S_{\kappa_i}(u\mid X_{it})+\frac{.003}{15}.
\end{equation}
The matched global and fully incremental models fix $\kappa$ at zero and one, respectively; the matched plan-guided model estimates a shared $\kappa$; the model with participant-specific incremental-choice weights estimates $\kappa_i$.
All models retain participant hierarchies for pragmatic optimality and stable-order weight, with the remaining task coefficients shared across participants.

\section{Production Model Comparison and Diagnostics}
\label{sec:appendix:model-diagnostics}

\subsection{Inference and Predictive Comparison}

Each likelihood evaluation scores all fifteen completed utterances and performs the required semantic composition and prefix-level normalisation.
To make this exact calculation feasible, we precomputed trial-independent utterance and prefix structures and vectorised and compiled the listener and speaker computations in JAX.
All eight production fits used four No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) chains on a GPU and 1{,}000 warm-up iterations per chain.
The two global models retained 4{,}000 draws per chain at maximum tree depth 8 to obtain adequate mixing; the other six models retained 1{,}000 draws per chain at depth 6.
The eight fits had zero divergences, maximum $\widehat R<1.01$, minimum bulk and tail effective sample sizes above 400, minimum Bayesian fraction of missing information (BFMI) above $.64$, and no observation with Pareto $k>.7$.
The participant-weight plan-guided fits reached the depth-6 step cap on $77.7\%$ of transitions under fixed semantics and $31.7\%$ under updating; these frequent depth-limit hits indicate restricted sampling efficiency, alongside the convergence and effective-sample-size values reported above.
The best-predicting context-fixed model had an absolute ELPD of $-13{,}039.924$, maximum $\widehat R=1.0051$, and minimum BFMI of $.742$.

Implementation checks verified that each modifier is applied once from the noun outwards and that changing the semantic regime can change predictions at matched parameters.
Endpoint, interpolation, and direct-versus-precomputed probability checks agreed within numerical precision, and recomputed posterior log likelihoods agreed with the stored values within $10^{-8}$.

The pointwise leave-one-out score vectors use the same trial ordering and response support.
Because observations are repeated within participants, paired score contributions are first summed within participant.
A participant-level Bayesian bootstrap then assigns Dirichlet weights to the 113 participant contributions over 200{,}000 draws.
Reported uncertainty comprises 95\% credible intervals and posterior probabilities for directional contrasts.
The predictive target is another response from a participant represented in the dataset, as described in \Cref{sec:model:inference}.

\section{Local Normalisation and Model Endpoints}
\label{sec:appendix:normalisation-audit}

\paragraph{Local adjective competition.}
For the utilities defined in \Cref{eq:prefix-raw-utility}, taking the logarithm of the product of locally normalised choices gives the accumulated utility minus the local-normalisation term.
Suppressing the shared referent and scene arguments,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \log \prod_t
  \frac{\exp U_t(w_t\mid u_{<t})}
       {\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(u_{<t})}\exp U_t(a\mid u_{<t})}
  &= \sum_t U_t(w_t\mid u_{<t})
     - \sum_t\log\!\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(u_{<t})}
       \exp U_t(a\mid u_{<t}) \\
  &= U^{\mathrm{complete}}(u)
     - \mathcal N^{\mathrm{local}}(u).
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:local-normalisation}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Relation between the endpoints.}
At common parameter values, plan-guided production with $\kappa=0$ exactly recovers global production, and with $\kappa=1$ exactly recovers fully incremental production.
The stable-order score, length and selection terms, and utterance space are identical across the three architectures.
Numerical checks found agreement in category probabilities and log probabilities within a tolerance of $10^{-10}$.

\paragraph{Plan-guided interpolation.}
Before the final Laplace smoothing, the same model can be written at common parameter values as a geometric interpolation between the global and fully incremental endpoints:
\begin{equation}
  S_{\kappa}(u\mid o^*,X)
  \propto
  S_{\mathrm{G}}(u\mid o^*,X)^{1-\kappa}
  S_{\mathrm{I}}(u\mid o^*,X)^{\kappa},
  \label{eq:plan-guided-interpolation}
\end{equation}
where $S_{\mathrm{G}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{I}}$ are the endpoint distributions evaluated with the same listener, stable-order component, length and selection terms, and parameter values.
The proportionality in \Cref{eq:plan-guided-interpolation} renormalises the interpolated scores over the fifteen completed utterances.


\section{Sequential Context Updating for Size}
\label{sec:appendix:semantic-details}

For the hierarchical semantic path, let $C_{j-1}$ be the context state after the syntactically inner modifiers have composed.
The semantic regime determines the comparison class used for size:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:hierarchical-size-regimes}
\begin{align}
  \llbracket \adj{S}\rrbracket_{\mathrm{fix}}(o)
  &=\llbracket \adj{S}\rrbracket_{\theta_{\delta}(\mathcal C)}(o),
  \label{eq:context-fixed}\\
  \llbracket \adj{S}\rrbracket_{\mathrm{upd},C_{j-1}}(o)
  &=\llbracket \adj{S}\rrbracket_{\theta_{\delta}(C_{j-1})}(o).
  \label{eq:context-updating}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\theta_{\delta}$ derives a graded size threshold from a comparison class and $\mathcal C$ is the original scene, represented by the uniform prior $P_0$.
The context-updating listener recomputes the size threshold from the hierarchically prior state; the context-fixed listener anchors it to the scene prior.
At each surface position, the production calculation evaluates the current prefix using this noun-outward interpretation.
Each prefix is interpreted from the original object prior, with each modifier applied once.

\section{Simulation Parameter Sweeps}
\label{sec:appendix:sim-params}

The original two-order simulation compares a global rule based on completed-utterance utility with an incremental rule that includes continuation-versus-stop competition.
We report its scene construction, definitions, and parameter sweeps first.
\Cref{sec:appendix:controlled-simulation} then introduces the production specification on the same scenes, as in the controlled comparison in \Cref{sec:simulation}.

Each random display contains between 2 and 30 objects whose sizes are drawn from a normal distribution centred at $15.5$ and truncated to $[1,30]$.
The largest object is designated as the target and assigned blue; each non-target object is independently assigned blue or non-blue with equal probability.
Three levels of contextual size spread vary how distinctly the largest target differs from its competitors.
The semantic grid varies colour reliability, size-threshold selectivity, and perceptual blur, which control the strength of colour-based restriction, how selectively \emph{big} applies, and how sharply size differences are represented.
In the simulation notation, $k$ corresponds to threshold selectivity $\delta$ and $w_f$ to perceptual blur $w$ in \Cref{eq:size-threshold,eq:size-membership}.

\Cref{fig:sim-k,fig:sim-wf,fig:sim-csv} show how the three fixed semantic parameters affect communicative success across the two speaker architectures.
Each figure separates the semantic regimes and averages equally over the other parameter settings.
For the two-order simulation, write $q(v,o)=L(o\mid v,X)^\alpha$, with $\alpha=1$ and equal utterance costs.
For distinct adjectives $a,b$, denote the original global and incremental rules by $S_G$ and $S_I$:
\begin{align}
 S_G(ab\mid o,X)&=\frac{q(ab,o)}{q(ab,o)+q(ba,o)},\\
 R_I(ab\mid o,X)&=\frac{q(a,o)}{q(a,o)+q(b,o)}
                  \frac{q(ab,o)}{q(ab,o)+q(a,o)},\\
 S_I(ab\mid o,X)&=\frac{R_I(ab\mid o,X)}{R_I(ab\mid o,X)+R_I(ba\mid o,X)}.
\end{align}
The second factor of $R_I$ is the continuation-versus-stop choice, and the final normalisation conditions on producing a completed two-adjective order.
The original global rule supplies the complete-utterance evaluation used in the first row of Panel A in the main comparison.
The production global model accumulates utility across adjective positions; its incremental endpoint assigns utterance length through the completed-response distribution.
The pragmatic listener uses a uniform referent prior, giving $L^{\mathrm{prag}}(o\mid ab,X)=S(ab\mid o,X)/\sum_r S(ab\mid r,X)$.
The implementation clips very small intermediate probabilities at $10^{-20}$.

The reproducible sweep uses seed 20260906 and samples 1{,}000 independent scenes for each context-size and size-spread combination.
Each scene is reused across semantic parameters, semantic regimes, and speakers, yielding 24{,}000 independent scenes and 12{,}000{,}000 model evaluations.
Monte Carlo standard errors are calculated after averaging parameter settings within scenes; repeated evaluations of one scene do not count as independent samples.
The computations use CUDA and 64-bit arithmetic.

\subsection{Supplementary Predictions on Experimental Displays}
\label{sec:appendix:fitted-bridge}

The supplementary simulation uses the 3{,}034 size--colour trials and all eight fitted production models.
For each model, 50 evenly spaced retained draws from each of its four chains give 200 posterior parameter draws.
The simulation preserves the fifteen named responses, fitted semantic constants, participant parameters, stable-order score, salience calculation, and length and selection terms.
For each trial and posterior draw, each of the six objects is evaluated as the intended referent while the adjective extensions and observed task covariates remain fixed.
Thus changing the candidate referent does not change which colour or form the uttered words denote.
Because the production responses were coded by adjective type, fixing these named adjective extensions across candidate referents is an additional assumption of the listener calculation.
This is a model-based extension to counterfactual referents within the observed task context.

Let $S_{it}^{(d)}(u\mid o,X)$ be the resulting complete-response probability at posterior draw $d$.
We evaluate the speaker's size-first probability conditional on the size--colour adjective set as
\begin{equation}
 \frac{S_{it}^{(d)}(\mathrm{SC}\mid o^*,X)}
 {S_{it}^{(d)}(\mathrm{SC}\mid o^*,X)+S_{it}^{(d)}(\mathrm{CS}\mid o^*,X)}.
\end{equation}
Separately, the pragmatic listener normalises the full-response speaker probabilities over the six referents using a uniform prior.
The listener therefore retains the fitted fifteen-response normalisation when evaluating each order.
We average each quantity over the original size--colour trials within posterior draw, then report the posterior mean and central 95\% interval.
Replaying the original target reproduces its stored likelihood within $10^{-8}$ for every model.

\Cref{fig:fitted-simulation-bridge} reports the resulting speaker and listener predictions.
In the participant-weight plan-guided models, the mean size-first target-probability advantage was $.0062$ under fixed semantics, 95\% posterior interval $[.0047,.0077]$, and $.0058$ under updating, $[.0045,.0072]$.
The corresponding mean probabilities of size-first conditional on the size--colour adjective set were $.967$ and $.966$.
Both fitted regimes therefore generate a size-first advantage under this counterfactual listener calculation.
In the response-score decomposition, the predictive difference between these models was concentrated in adjective content and length.
The two calculations describe how the models differ in predicting the choice of a description while both assigning a listener advantage to size-first order.
